Start Here: What Is AST SpaceMobile?

AST SpaceMobile is a company building something that has never existed before: a mobile phone network in space. Not a satellite internet service that requires a dish. Not a satellite phone that costs thousands of pounds. A network that works with the ordinary smartphone already in your pocket - the same iPhone or Samsung you use every day - using the same SIM card, the same phone number, the same carrier.

Founded in 2017 by Abel Avellan, a serial telecommunications entrepreneur, and headquartered in Midland, Texas, the company has spent nine years building the technology to make this possible. As of mid-2026, they have satellites in orbit, paying commercial customers, $3.5 billion in cash, and agreements with nearly 60 of the world's largest mobile networks covering an estimated 3 to 6 billion people.

The Problem

Why Do Mobile Dead Zones Still Exist?

Mobile phone coverage works through ground-based towers. Each tower covers a radius of a few miles. When you're in a city, you're never far from a tower - so you always have signal. But towers are expensive to build and maintain, and they only make financial sense where there are enough paying customers nearby to justify the cost.

That means vast parts of the world have no signal at all. Rural areas. Oceans. Mountain ranges. Deserts. Developing countries where the economics don't work for traditional networks. Nearly 500,000 square miles of US land alone are classified as dead zones. Globally, an estimated 6 billion mobile phones experience regular connectivity gaps - moments where you have no signal and cannot make a call, send a message, or access the internet.

Think of it this way: Traditional mobile networks are like a grid of street lamps. They light up areas where it makes economic sense to put a lamp. But huge stretches of the world - the wilderness, the sea, rural communities in Africa and Asia - are left in the dark. AST SpaceMobile is building a sun: a light source so high up and so powerful that it illuminates everywhere, all at once.

The Technology

How Does a Satellite Signal Reach Your Phone?

The key challenge - the one that's stumped the industry for decades - is that ordinary smartphones aren't designed to communicate with satellites. They're built for ground towers a few miles away. Satellites are hundreds of miles up. Getting a signal that far has always required specialist hardware: the large dishes you see on Starlink homes, or the bulky handsets of satellite phone services like Iridium.

AST SpaceMobile's breakthrough is the BlueBird satellite. Each BlueBird carries a massive phased-array antenna - essentially a large, flat panel of thousands of tiny antenna elements working together. BlueBird 6, the largest commercial communications array ever deployed in orbit, is roughly the size of a basketball court. This enormous antenna is powerful enough to send and receive 4G/5G signals directly to and from a standard smartphone on the ground, even though the satellite is orbiting at 450km altitude travelling at 17,000mph.

Think of it this way: A normal satellite is like shouting to someone a mile away - they can't hear you. AST SpaceMobile's BlueBird is like fitting a giant megaphone and amplifier that makes the shout so powerful it carries the full distance, and the person on the ground (your phone) doesn't need any special equipment to hear it.

The company has already demonstrated this works. In 2025, BlueBird satellites achieved 98.9 Mbps peak data speeds directly to an unmodified smartphone over open water - that's faster than many home broadband connections. Their next-generation Block 2 satellites are expected to nearly double that. Voice calls, video calls, data - all working from space, to a normal phone, without any additional hardware.

The Business Model

How Does AST SpaceMobile Actually Make Money?

This is where the business model becomes genuinely elegant. AST SpaceMobile is not a mobile network operator. It does not sell SIM cards, phone plans, or devices. It does not compete with AT&T, Vodafone, or T-Mobile. Instead, it sells connectivity wholesale to those carriers, who then offer it to their existing customers as an add-on service.

Here's how it works in practice: You're an AT&T customer. You drive out of town and lose signal. Normally, your phone would show “No Service.” With ASTS technology activated, instead your phone seamlessly connects via one of AST's satellites overhead, and your screen might show “AT&T via Satellite.” You can still make calls and access data. AT&T charges you a small premium for this capability and shares a portion of that revenue with AST SpaceMobile.

Think of it this way: AST SpaceMobile is the wholesale electricity generator. The mobile carriers - AT&T, Vodafone, Verizon - are the power retailers that sell it to consumers. AST provides the infrastructure, the carriers provide the customers, and both share the revenue. This model means AST never has to acquire a single customer directly - their partners already have billions of them.

This is strategically important. It means AST SpaceMobile has no customer acquisition cost and no churn risk at the subscriber level. The carriers absorb those costs. AST simply needs to keep satellites in orbit, which is a fixed cost. As more people use the service, revenue scales dramatically - but operating costs barely move. This is why analysts project EBITDA margins approaching 90% once the constellation reaches full operational scale. Once the satellites are up, almost every additional pound of revenue flows directly to profit.

Why Not Just Use Starlink?

How Is ASTS Different from Starlink?

Starlink, SpaceX's satellite internet service, is the most well-known name in space-based connectivity. But it works very differently to AST SpaceMobile, and the two products serve different markets rather than competing head-to-head for the same customers.

Starlink requires hardware. To use Starlink, you need to buy a dish terminal (currently around $599 in the US), power it, and point it at the sky. This works brilliantly for homes, boats, and vehicles in remote areas - but it doesn't work for the 6 billion people who just want their existing mobile phone to have signal everywhere. Starlink is a fixed broadband substitute. AST SpaceMobile is a mobile network extension.

Starlink competes with carriers. SpaceX sells Starlink directly to consumers, which means it is in direct competition with the very telecoms companies that AST SpaceMobile partners with. Carriers have little incentive to integrate Starlink deeply - it's a rival. They have every incentive to integrate AST SpaceMobile - it's an extension of their own service that makes them more competitive and generates incremental revenue.

AST SpaceMobile's peak data speeds exceed Starlink Direct-to-Cell. SpaceX has its own direct-to-cell service in development, but AST's demonstrated 98.9 Mbps to an unmodified phone is ahead of what Starlink Direct-to-Cell has publicly demonstrated for full data services. AST's 3,900+ patent portfolio - covering the core phased-array technology - creates meaningful IP barriers to replication.

The key distinction: Starlink says “buy our product instead of your carrier.” AST SpaceMobile says “let us make your carrier's product work everywhere.” Carriers strongly prefer the second option.

Why the Carrier Model Is the Smartest Part of This Thesis

Many investors initially compare ASTS to Starlink and assume it's a head-to-head satellite internet race. It's not. The reason ASTS commands such a high market valuation relative to its current revenue is the structural elegance of its carrier partnership model.

Consider what AST SpaceMobile has already built, without yet having a full constellation: agreements with AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile in the US (covering virtually all US mobile subscribers), Vodafone across Europe and Africa, Rakuten in Japan (a 50/50 joint venture with nationwide service targeted for 2027), stc Group across the Middle East with a $175 million prepayment already received, and a further 50+ operators globally spanning almost every continent. These are not letters of intent. Many are signed commercial agreements with minimum revenue commitments. The $1.2 billion in contracted backlog represents money ASTS has effectively already earned - it just needs to get the satellites up to collect it.

The carrier model also creates a near-irreplaceable competitive moat. Each carrier has integrated or is integrating ASTS's technology into their own network infrastructure. As this integration deepens - with ASTS's signals appearing seamlessly in carrier apps, customer accounts, and billing systems - switching costs rise. A carrier that has spent two years integrating AST technology is not going to rip it out to try a competitor. The first-mover advantage in carrier relationships compounds over time.

The US Government Angle: A Second Revenue Engine

There is a third dimension to the revenue story that most retail investors overlook. AST SpaceMobile's satellites are capable of providing secure, resilient communications in areas where no terrestrial infrastructure exists - exactly the capability the US military and defence agencies need. The company has already won three new US Government awards since March 2026 through prime contractors, a $30 million prime contract from the Space Development Agency (HALO Europa programme), and a prime position on the US Missile Defense Agency's SHIELD programme.

Government contracts are structurally different from commercial carrier revenue: they are milestone-based, often multi-year, and tend to grow as relationships deepen. CEO Abel Avellan has explicitly framed the government market as having “significant upside depending on certain contract outcomes” in 2027. If ASTS becomes a dual-use commercial and defence communication platform, the revenue ceiling rises substantially above the $1B 2027 guidance.

The EBITDA margin story: Once a satellite constellation is in orbit, the operating costs are largely fixed - ground station maintenance, staff, insurance, spectrum fees. Revenue, however, scales with subscriber usage and carrier payments. This means that as ASTS grows from $200M in 2026 revenue toward $1B in 2027 and beyond, the incremental revenue increasingly falls straight to the bottom line. Analysts project EBITDA margins approaching 90% at full operational scale - comparable to the world's best SaaS businesses. This is the reason ASTS carries a market cap of $22B on $70M of 2025 revenue: the market is valuing those future high-margin cash flows, not today's numbers.

Manufacturing Progress: The Pipeline Is Real

One of the most frequent sceptic arguments about ASTS is whether the manufacturing ambition is realistic. The evidence suggests it is. The company operates over 500,000 square feet of manufacturing and operations space globally, with 95% vertical integration - meaning they build almost everything in-house. A dedicated micron production facility in Texas is now fully operational, producing components for more than 10 satellites' worth of phased arrays per month. Assembly is already complete for satellites through BlueBird 28, with production continuing through BlueBird 32+. The manufacturing pipeline is running meaningfully ahead of the launch schedule.

The Blue Origin Saga: Execution Risk Made Visible

In 2026, AST SpaceMobile's share price has been one of the most volatile in the market - partly because of genuine progress, and partly because of two incidents involving its launch partner Blue Origin that reminded investors this is not a theoretical risk story. Execution risk is real, it affects the timeline, and the share price reacts sharply when it materialises.

April 19, 2026 - Primary Incident

BlueBird 7 Deposited in Wrong Orbit

AST SpaceMobile's BlueBird 7 - its second Block 2 next-generation satellite - launched on Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket (NG-3) from Cape Canaveral. The rocket performed correctly during launch, but the satellite deployment stage placed BlueBird 7 into an orbit too low to maintain station. The satellite's onboard propulsion system could not compensate for the deficit. BlueBird 7 was declared lost and will be de-orbited. Financial impact: $155–160M asset write-off in Q2 2026, covered by insurance policy. Satellite programme impact: One satellite lost, no structural damage to the broader programme, as manufacture was already ahead through BlueBird 32. Launch timeline impact: Modest - the next launch (BB 8, 9, 10) was already planned on SpaceX Falcon 9.

ASTS stock fell 15% at open on April 20

May 28–29, 2026 - Secondary Incident

Blue Origin New Glenn “Catastrophic” Explosion During Static Fire Test

Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket suffered an explosion at Launch Complex 36, Cape Canaveral during a scheduled static fire test ahead of its fourth planned mission (which was to carry Amazon Project Kuiper satellites). The upper stage failed. LC-36 was damaged. Analysts at Deutsche Bank warned the incident could delay Blue Origin's launch schedule by as much as six months, potentially pushing AST's deployment timeline from Q4 2026 into Q1 2027. Roth Capital maintained its Buy rating but cut price target to $108 from $137. Deutsche Bank downgraded the stock. However: AST's next scheduled launch (BlueBird 8, 9, 10) was already planned on SpaceX Falcon 9 and was not affected. ASTS management maintained their 2026 guidance and stated they had “sufficient contracted launch capacity” across multiple providers.

ASTS stock fell 14-18% pre-market on May 29

June 17, 2026 - Recovery

BlueBird 8, 9 and 10 Successfully Launched and Operational

All three Block 2 next-generation satellites launched successfully on SpaceX Falcon 9 and were confirmed operational in orbit. The deployment validates that the Block 2 platform performs as expected, that AST's multi-provider launch strategy provides resilience, and that manufacturing output is sufficient to maintain cadence despite the BlueBird 7 loss. Each Block 2 satellite is designed to nearly double the peak data speeds achieved by Block 1 satellites.

ASTS stock rose 19-22% on June 29 confirmation

What the Blue Origin incidents actually mean for the thesis: The short-term share price reaction was severe. The long-term impact is more nuanced. ASTS lost one satellite (insured), faced potential six-month delays to Blue Origin launches, but (a) has SpaceX as a launch alternative, (b) maintained 2026 guidance, and (c) had manufacturing output already running ahead of its launch schedule. UBS analyst Christopher Schoell put it clearly: “The financial impact on AST will be limited, but ASTS and its share price are now linked with Blue Origin's New Glenn reliability.” This is the correct read. The thesis is not broken. But launch dependency on a relatively young, not-yet-proven-at-scale rocket from Blue Origin is a genuine ongoing risk that investors must price in.

The most important insight in the ASTS vs RKLB debate is that they are not competitors - they are complementary plays in the space economy. RKLB is the infrastructure layer (rockets, satellite buses). ASTS is the network layer (connectivity services). In fact, Rocket Lab could theoretically become a launch provider for future ASTS satellites. Holding both in a portfolio is not a contradiction - it is a way to own different risk profiles within the same structural theme.

From a valuation standpoint, the Motley Fool analysis from March 2026 identified that ASTS trades at approximately 16x its 2028 estimated sales, compared to RKLB at 26x. If both companies execute, ASTS offers a steeper discount to its forward revenue. But RKLB has already proven revenue execution and a clearer path. The choice between them is a choice between “higher ceiling, more execution risk” (ASTS) and “more certain runway, slightly lower multiple upside” (RKLB).

Risks: The Full Picture

Launch dependency Dilution history +437% Pre-profitability Starlink Direct-to-Cell Timeline slippage Insider selling Capital intensity

Launch dependency is the single most immediate risk. ASTS needs to put roughly 36 more satellites in orbit by year-end to reach its 45-satellite continuous service target. That requires multiple successful launches from a combination of SpaceX Falcon 9 and Blue Origin New Glenn. Blue Origin has now suffered two significant failures involving ASTS payloads or its own infrastructure in under two months. If Blue Origin is sidelined for an extended period due to the LC-36 pad damage, the constellation deployment timeline slips by months - which delays commercial service activation and pushes the $1B 2027 revenue target further into uncertainty.

Dilution has been substantial and may continue. Over the past five years, AST SpaceMobile's share count has grown by approximately 437%. The company raised $1.075 billion in new convertible notes in February 2026 (with a conversion price of $116.30). Each capital raise is dilutive to existing shareholders. As long as ASTS is pre-profitability, it will periodically need to raise additional capital. The $3.5B cash position provides visibility through the initial constellation build, but future tranches remain possible.

Insider selling has been notable. Over $270 million in shares were offloaded by insiders in the 90 days prior to April 2026, including a $116 million sale by a major shareholder. Insiders selling while publicly promoting the company's long-term outlook is a caution signal that investors should weigh.

Starlink Direct-to-Cell is a real competitor. SpaceX has begun deploying its own direct-to-device satellite service, initially for text messaging and voice through T-Mobile in the US. While ASTS's demonstrated data speeds exceed what Starlink D2C has publicly shown, SpaceX has unmatched manufacturing scale, launch cadence, and capital resources. If Starlink D2C catches up technologically, some of ASTS's carrier partners could have an alternative option.

The 45-satellite timeline may already be at risk. Analyst William Blair noted after the April Blue Origin failure that the 45-satellite year-end target “will likely be hard to hit.” Deutsche Bank's post-May explosion analysis expected a ~6-month delay to Blue Origin launches. If the end-of-2026 goal becomes a H1-2027 goal, the $1B 2027 revenue target becomes a 2028 target - which compresses the return timeline and may require additional capital raises.

The SpaceOdysseyHub deep-dive from April 2026 framed this perfectly: “AST SpaceMobile is an event-driven stock through 2026. Every 45-day launch window, every quarterly revenue print, every commercial-service milestone is a binary information event that resolves the bull/bear gap. The question is not whether direct-to-cell satellite service is a real business - it is. The question is whether AST is the operator that captures the largest share of it.” This is exactly the right frame for a long-term investor. The opportunity is real. The execution risk is real. Position accordingly.

Bottom Line: The Biggest Connectivity Bet in Public Markets

AST SpaceMobile is attempting something no company has ever done at commercial scale: build a space-based mobile network that works with every smartphone on earth, through every major carrier, without requiring any new hardware. If that statement sounds impossible, the fact that they have already demonstrated 98.9 Mbps to an unmodified phone from orbit, with 60 carrier agreements, $3.5B in cash, and the first commercial satellites operating right now, should reframe your view of what “possible” means here.

The structural thesis: 6 billion mobile phones experience connectivity gaps. A wholesale satellite network selling through existing carriers has no customer acquisition cost and near-zero incremental operating cost once deployed. At full scale, EBITDA margins approach 90%. The $22B market cap is the market pricing in a roughly 50/50 probability that ASTS captures a major share of a $100B+ addressable market. Investors who believe the probability is higher than 50/50 should be long.

The near-term reality: The 2026 story is about satellite deployment, not revenue. Watch launch cadence from both SpaceX and Blue Origin. Watch the 45-satellite year-end target. Watch Q3 and Q4 revenue prints for the commercial activation ramp. Watch the Japan Rakuten JV for the first large-scale international service launch. Each of these is a binary catalyst that will move the stock sharply in one direction or the other.

The Blue Origin risk: This is the most important near-term variable. Two incidents in under two months from the same launch vehicle is a pattern investors cannot ignore. The thesis does not require Blue Origin - SpaceX Falcon 9 is the primary vehicle and it has performed - but delays to the New Glenn manifest could push continuous service activation from Q4 2026 into 2027, which delays the revenue inflection. Know this risk and size accordingly.
