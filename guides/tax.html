The Tax Wrapper Guide

Every ISA, pension and account type: what each one does with tax on the way in, while the money grows and on the way out, and the order people often use them in.

      Part 2

      What a wrapper does

      A wrapper is the account the money sits in, not the investment itself. The same fund can be held in an ISA, a pension or an ordinary account. What changes is how tax treats it.

      1Going in
      2While it grows
      3Coming out

      Tax can reach money at three points. Going in: is anything added, such as tax relief or a bonus? While it grows: is there tax on gains, dividends or interest? Coming out: is the money taxed, and when can it be reached? Each wrapper is a different set of answers to those three questions.

      An ISA adds nothing on the way in, but nothing is taxed while the money grows or when it comes out. A pension adds tax relief on the way in and charges no tax while the money grows, but most of it is taxed as income on the way out, and it cannot usually be reached before 55. An ordinary investment account helps at none of the three points.

      Part 3

      Your options

      Each wrapper treats tax differently and has its own rules on access. Most people use more than one. Which ones, and in which order, depends on their situation.

      ISA

      Cash ISA

      Tax-free interest on savings

      Starting out
      £20,000a year, shared across all ISAs
      Interest is tax-freeUsually easy accessNo investment growth

      A savings account where the interest is free of tax. It is often used for an emergency fund, or for money needed within a few years, where keeping the value steady matters more than growth. Over long periods, inflation can wear away what the cash will buy.

      More detail
      Often used for
      • An emergency fund, often three to six months of spending
      • Money needed within about three years
      • Savers whose interest outside an ISA would go over their personal savings allowance
      From 6 April 2027
      • People under 65 can put up to £12,000 a year into Cash ISAs. The rest of the £20,000 can still go into other ISAs, such as a Stocks & Shares ISA.
      • People under 65 can no longer move money from a Stocks & Shares ISA into a Cash ISA.
      • People aged 65 and over keep the full £20,000 for cash.
      Savings outside an ISA
      Basic rate

      Higher rate
      £500 a year tax-free
      Additional rate
      No allowance

      Lifetime ISA

      ISA

      Lifetime ISA

      A 25% bonus for a first home or for later life

      Starting out
      £4,000a year, inside the £20,000 ISA allowance
      25% bonus, up to £1,000 a yearNo tax on growth25% charge on other withdrawals

      The government adds 25% to what goes in, up to £1,000 a year. The money can be used without a charge towards a first home costing up to £450,000, or taken out from age 60. Taken out for any other reason, it loses 25% of the amount withdrawn, which is more than the bonus added. It can be held as cash or invested.

      More detail
      Who can use one
      Open
      From 18 to 39
      Pay in
      Until age 50
      Taking money out without a charge
      First home
      Up to £450,000, at least 12 months after the first payment
      Age 60 or over

      Terminal illness
      Less than 12 months to live
      Cash or invested
      • A cash Lifetime ISA keeps the value steady and earns interest. It is often used when the purchase is a few years away.
      • A Stocks & Shares Lifetime ISA is invested, so it can grow more over long periods and can also fall. It is often used when the goal is more than five years away, or for later life.
      A replacement
      • The government consulted in 2026 on replacing the Lifetime ISA with a First Time Buyer ISA. No start date has been confirmed. Until then, Lifetime ISAs can still be opened and paid into under the current rules.

      Pension

      SIPP

      A self-invested personal pension

      Growing +
      £60,000a year across all pensions, or 100% of earnings if lower
      Tax relief on the way inNo tax while it growsLocked until 55, then 57

      A personal pension where the saver chooses the provider and the investments. Contributions get tax relief: a basic-rate taxpayer who pays in £800 ends up with £1,000 in the pension, because the provider claims £200 from the government. Higher and additional-rate taxpayers can claim more back through a tax return.

      Limits
      • Personal contributions get relief on up to 100% of UK earnings a year, or £3,600 including relief for people with little or no earnings.
      • Unused annual allowance from the previous three tax years can be carried forward, for someone who was in a registered pension scheme in those years. In 2026/27 that allows up to £240,000 in one year (this year's £60,000 plus three years of £60,000), still within the earnings limit for personal contributions.
      • The annual allowance is reduced for people whose income, including pension contributions, is above £260,000, by £1 for every £2 over, down to £10,000. This only applies when income without pension contributions is also above £200,000.

      An ordinary investment account, often called a GIA. There is no limit on what goes in, and no shelter from tax: gains above the yearly capital gains tax allowance and dividends above the dividend allowance can be taxed. It is often used for money above what ISAs and pensions can take.

      How people reduce the tax
      • Bed and ISA: selling investments in the account and buying them back inside an ISA, up to the ISA allowance. Future growth is then sheltered; any gain made on the sale counts for capital gains tax.
      • The yearly allowance: taking gains of up to £3,000 each year, since the allowance does not carry over.
      • Losses: a loss made on a sale can be set against gains in the same tax year, and unused losses can be carried forward once reported to HMRC.
      • Spouses and civil partners: investments can pass between them without capital gains tax, so each can use their own allowances.

      Part 4

      The order people often use

      There is no single right order. But many people work through their options in roughly the same sequence, and each step sits where it does for a reason.

      Tap a step for the reason behind it.

      1Workplace pension, up to the employer matchThe employer's extra money only arrives if the employee pays in.

      On top of the employer's money, tax relief is added, so each pound of take-home pay puts more than a pound into the pension. The trade-off is the lock: the money cannot usually be reached until 55, or 57 from 6 April 2028.

      2A cash emergency fundMoney that can be reached quickly, without selling investments at a bad time.

      Often three to six months of spending, held in an easy-access account or a Cash ISA. It sits early in the order because the later steps work best when they are not raided for emergencies.

      3An ISA or Lifetime ISATax-free growth, with the money reachable at any time, or a 25% bonus.

      A Stocks & Shares ISA keeps money reachable for goals before retirement. A Lifetime ISA adds 25% for a first home or for later life, but it has to be opened before 40 and charges 25% on other withdrawals.

      4More into a pensionThe relief is larger for higher-rate taxpayers.

      A higher-rate taxpayer gets 40% relief going in, and many pay a lower rate of tax when the money comes out. The cost is the same lock as step 1, so this step tends to come after money that might be needed sooner.

      5A general investment accountFor money above what ISAs and pensions can take.

      It comes last because it has no tax shelter. Using the yearly capital gains and dividend allowances, and moving money into an ISA each April, keeps the tax down.

      What changes the order

      Expensive debt, such as a credit card, is often cleared before investing. A first home within a few years moves cash or a Lifetime ISA up the list. Being close to 55 (57 from 6 April 2028) makes the pension lock matter less; being decades away makes it matter more. A higher-rate taxpayer gets more from pension relief than a basic-rate one. Each of these is a reason two people on the same income can use a different order.

      Part 5

      What is changing

      Changes already in law, with the date each one starts, and one proposal still being decided.

      1. From 6 April 2027Cash ISA limit for under-65s falls to £12,000

        The overall ISA allowance stays at £20,000, and the rest can go into other ISAs. People under 65 can no longer move money from a Stocks & Shares ISA into a Cash ISA, and interest on uninvested cash inside a Stocks & Shares ISA is charged at a flat rate.

      2. From 6 April 2027Tax on savings interest rises by 2 percentage points

        Interest outside an ISA above the personal savings allowance is taxed at 22%, 42% or 47%, up from 20%, 40% and 45%. The allowances themselves do not change.

      3. From 6 April 2027Unused pensions count for inheritance tax

        Most pension money left unused at death counts towards the estate for inheritance tax. Money left to a spouse or civil partner is still exempt.

      4. From 6 April 2028The earliest pension age rises to 57

        The normal minimum age for taking money from a personal or workplace pension rises from 55 to 57. Some schemes have a protected lower age.

      5. From 6 April 2029National Insurance saving on salary sacrifice is capped

        Only the first £2,000 a year sacrificed into a pension is free of National Insurance. Income tax relief still applies to the whole contribution.

      6. No date yetA replacement for the Lifetime ISA

        The government consulted in 2026 on a First Time Buyer ISA to replace the Lifetime ISA. Its details and start date are expected at a future Budget. Until then, Lifetime ISAs can still be opened and paid into.

      Summary

      01

      A wrapper changes the tax, not the investment.

      The same fund can sit in an ISA, a pension or an ordinary account. Tax treats each one differently going in, while it grows and coming out.

      02

      ISAs are simple and flexible.

      Nothing is added on the way in, but nothing is taxed later, and the money can be reached at any time.

      03

      Pensions add the most on the way in.

      Tax relief and employer money top up each pound, in exchange for locking it away until 55, or 57 from 6 April 2028, and taxing most of it on the way out.

      04

      Most allowances reset every April.

      The ISA, capital gains and dividend allowances do not carry over, so what is unused in one tax year is gone. Only unused pension allowance can be carried forward.
