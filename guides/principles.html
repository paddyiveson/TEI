Part 1

Why invest at all?

Cash keeps its number but loses what it can buy. Investing is the answer to that, as long as the money stays invested.

A bank balance can stay the same for years while what it buys shrinks. Prices rise a little every year, so cash loses buying power quietly, without the number on the statement ever falling.

Investing answers this by putting money into productive assets that can grow faster than prices. Growth on top of growth is compounding, the biggest lever an ordinary investor has. How wealth compounds shows the curve it draws.

The gap only opens if the money stays invested. Buying today and selling in six weeks is speculation wearing investing's clothes. Long-term ownership gives compounding the years it needs.

Part 2

How should I think?

Most people default to one of two habits. Neither is a strategy.

The passive default puts everything into a broad tracker, on autopilot, without weighing it against anything else. Chasing the noise means buying whatever is trending this week. Both hand the decision to something other than thinking.

Successful investing is behavioural before it is technical. Patience, discipline and a longer time horizon consistently beat speed, activity and being early to a headline.

The test is simple to state. Does this change the business, or only the mood around it? Earnings and execution are signal. Headlines and single red days are mostly noise, although a real change can arrive dressed as an ordinary headline.

Part 3

How do we think?

The same discipline, applied on every decision so it does not depend on willpower in the moment.

That discipline is easy to describe and hard to hold alone, especially when a headline says panic. This way of thinking was refined over years of expensive mistakes: over-investing when timing was wrong, sitting out when conviction should have meant acting, chasing stories that had already peaked.

The real mispricing rarely sits in the names everyone already owns. It sits in structural shifts the market has not fully priced, because the payoff is years away, not months. That is where patient capital has room to compound.

What earns a place: a strong story with structural tailwinds, numbers that confirm the thesis, and leadership worth backing. What does not: excitement, momentum, or the feeling that everyone else is already in.

Conviction and market favour are separate, and they do not move together. The largest positions are not simply the highest-conviction ideas. They are the ideas where high conviction and current market favour overlap.

MMarcus only buys names already in the news, so he tends to pay the top-right price. Why investors buy at the top.

PPriya builds quietly in the top left, and is already positioned when the crowd arrives.

Conviction and market favour rotate independently, and that rotation is where the discipline pays. Money held quietly through a period of low favour, while the thesis keeps strengthening, is what earns the sharp re-rating when the market catches up.

The principle also runs the other way. Once a position has been re-rated and sits high-conviction, high-favour, its gains are not automatically safe. Rotating some of that gain into the next high-conviction name still out of favour protects what has been made, rather than leaving it in a name that is due to cool.

The discipline is not "buy and hold forever". It is holding through the quiet phase, then moving on once the crowd has arrived. Why we sell winners too early covers the opposite mistake.

Part 4

Building a portfolio

How do these principles become a portfolio?

Every portfolio we build has a Growth sleeve and a Foundation sleeve, working together. Exact weightings depend on the person; what matters here is the structure. Growth is the engine and Foundation is the cushion.

Within Growth, the tiers size by conviction: Anchor, Core and Tail. Within Foundation, broad markets do the steady work. Tap a tier for more.

A position's effect on the portfolio depends on two things: how much it gains, and how much of the portfolio it is. A name that doubles is a big win on its own, but at 2% of the portfolio the overall result barely moves.

Spreading too thin, too early, across too many names is the most common way good ideas end up contributing almost nothing. The Anchor, Core and Tail tiers exist to solve this: conviction earns a size large enough that the portfolio feels it when the thesis plays out.

It works both ways. A name that halves costs Priya more than three times what it costs Marcus, which is why size follows risk as well as conviction. Conviction should track belief in the story, not how large a position has grown; checking in honestly, position by position, keeps the two aligned.

Cash is not dead weight. It is optionality. A cash allocation of 5 to 10% exists for one purpose: to act decisively when conviction names pull back hard. Marcus and Priya each keep £1,000 as cash, about 9% of their £11,000.

Volatility and thesis failure look similar in the moment, and they call for opposite responses. Telling them apart starts with one question: has the story changed, or only the price?

Response framework

The index and individual holdings do not always move together. Money rotates between sectors and themes, and growth names move more than the index (higher beta). Three patterns are worth telling apart.

Rotation Down 5 to 10%, no news

Single names or a cluster fall while the index does not.

The index may be flat or even up while a handful of holdings, or a group of similar names, are down 5 to 10% with no company-specific reason. This is usually money rotating between sectors or themes, not a change in the story. Response: nibble. Add a small amount to names already held with conviction, and keep most of the cash back, since rotation can last longer than it feels like it should.

High beta Down 10 to 20%

Growth names fall harder than the index during macro stress.

The index falls a few percent, but higher-beta growth names fall much more as money moves towards safer assets. Response: more than a nibble. Deploy more into the highest-conviction names, provided the story has not changed, and still hold some cash back in case conditions worsen, unless more contributions are due soon.

Sustained shock Prolonged drawdown

A serious macro event drives an extended fall in growth names.

Occasionally the index falls modestly while growth names fall heavily and stay down for a long period. This is the moment to deploy with real conviction, concentrated in the leading names already in the largest-positions quadrant, and to be patient. Positions built here, in genuine conviction rather than in everything at once, are typically what benefit most when the market turns.

PPriya's £1,000 goes in as £250 on a rotation and £500 if it becomes a high-beta fall, with £250 still held back if it turns into something worse.

MMarcus puts all £1,000 in on the first red day, afraid of missing the bottom. If it falls further, he has nothing left to add.

Before adding on any fall, the question is whether the story has changed, not just the number. A lower price is not automatically an opportunity.

Part 5

Living the principles

How does someone remain a successful investor over decades?

The first 18 months build the machine. What comes after decides whether it keeps running.

By month 18, Marcus and Priya have each paid in £13,600, and at an even 7% a year their pots would be worth about £14,847. Almost all of that is still their own saving, which is why the first £10,000 feels so slow.

Same starting principles, same money. Marcus checks daily, reacts to price and rotates on headlines. Priya reviews on a fixed schedule and acts only when the thesis changes, not the price.

Each time Marcus sells on a headline and waits to feel sure before buying back, his money sits in cash and misses that stretch of growth. Over 30 years, the gap between them is not luck. It is behaviour, repeated long enough to compound.

The gap is not certain either way. Time out of the market can also miss a fall; missing the best days shows what real market history says about the trade.

There is no point where the work is finished, and no point where every move needs attention. The discipline is finding the steady middle, and holding it.TEI Principles

What this does not say. This guide sets out how we think about investing. It does not say what anyone holds, how much or when: that depends on goals, time horizon, existing savings and how much risk a person can live with. Marcus and Priya are illustrations, not real people, and their figures assume an even 7% a year and prices rising 2.5% a year, before fees and tax; real returns vary and can be negative for years. Holding fewer names raises the chance of large losses as well as large gains. The historical examples are hindsight: they show the principles at work, not a way to find the next one.
