Master the principles, ignore the noise

A market headline and a real change in a business are not the same thing. A rule set in advance helps tell them apart.

An old radio, tuned between stations, produces static. Somewhere inside that static sits a real broadcast: an actual signal, carrying real information. Markets work in a similar way. Underneath the static of daily price moves sits a much smaller amount of real information about what a business is worth.

Most of what moves a share price day to day is static: one analyst's forecast, a headline about a rate decision, one trader's mood. None of it changes what the business earns, owns or sells. It only changes what other people will pay for a small piece of it, for a few hours or days.

Signal is different. It is a change to the business itself: a shift in earnings power, a competitor gaining ground, a market growing or shrinking for real. Signal is rare and slow. Noise is constant and fast. Assuming everything is noise carries its own risk: a real change can arrive dressed as an ordinary headline.

An illustration, not a real share price. The value line steps up once, where the business genuinely changes; everything else the price does is static around it. Sorting a real headline into either list on the day is still hard, and getting it wrong in either direction has a cost.

Telling the two apart in real time is difficult. Emotion runs highest exactly when the static is loudest, the worst moment to invent a new rule from scratch. This is what a fixed principle is for: a rule written down in advance, then applied the same way however loud the static gets.

In the short run, the market is a voting machine, but in the long run, it is a weighing machine.Benjamin Graham

A rule set in advance is not infallible. A principle written years ago can turn stale if the business or the wider economy has changed. It only holds up if it is revisited against real evidence, not defended out of habit.

A move driven by noise can also open a gap between price and value, sometimes widest when things look bleakest, though not every bleak moment is noise. Spotting the difference is a skill built by studying how markets have behaved through moments like these, not by reacting to one headline.

What this does not say. None of this says whether today's headline is noise or a genuine change in a specific business. It shows the mechanism worth understanding either way: a rule decided in advance, and the pattern recognition that only comes from watching enough of these moments happen.

Summary

01

Price moves and business value answer different questions.

A share price can move on sentiment alone while nothing about the business changes.

02

A rule set in advance is a different kind of decision.

It is made with a clear head, before any outcome is known.

03

A rule that never bends is a blind spot.

Real, lasting change in a business is signal too, and a rule has to be able to recognise it.
