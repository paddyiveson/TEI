What happens if you miss the 10 best days in the market?

Most of a market's long-run gain arrives on a handful of days, and those days tend to land right next to the worst ones. Step out to avoid a crash and you usually miss the rebound too.

A year's rainfall does not arrive evenly. Much of it falls in a few heavy downpours, while most days bring a little drizzle or nothing at all. Leave the water butt covered on the wrong few days and it stays close to empty, however long it sat open the rest of the year. A share market's long-run gain works the same way.

The S&P 500, an index of 500 of the largest US companies, shows it clearly. Over the twenty years to the end of 2025 it traded on 5,029 days. Every one of them is a dot below.

0.2%of the trading days. Miss just these 10 and $10,000 grows to $35,865 instead of $80,619.
S&P 500 Total Return Index (dividends reinvested), in US dollars, 3 January 2006 to 31 December 2025. One dot per trading day, read left to right and top to bottom; the shading changes with each calendar year. "Best" means the largest one-day rise.

Those few dots carry a surprising share of twenty years' growth. The next figure takes them out one batch at a time: on each missed day the money earns nothing, and every other day it stays invested as normal.

S&P 500 Total Return Index, in US dollars: $10,000 invested at the close on 30 December 2005 and held to 31 December 2025. A missed day earns 0%, as if the money sat in cash without interest; every other day compounds. Before fees, taxes and currency moves. One historical stretch of the US market, not a forecast.

Ten days is about two weeks of trading spread across twenty years. Missing them took the end value from $80,619 to $35,865. Miss forty and the investor finishes with less than they started, over a period in which the market itself grew eightfold.

The obvious fix is to sidestep the bad days and keep the good ones. The trouble is where the good days sit.

7 of 10of the best days came within a month of one of the 10 worst. Six came within two weeks.
S&P 500 Total Return Index, daily moves. Best and worst are the 10 largest one-day rises and falls of 2006–2025; gaps are counted in trading days, and a month here is 21 trading days. The three best days not shown in a pair, 10 and 23 March 2009 and 9 April 2025, each followed a run of heavy falls that were just outside the worst 10.

This is why selling in a crash so often backfires. The biggest rises tend to come in the middle of the falls, often within days of the worst of them, while the news is at its most frightening. An investor who sells after a bad day and waits for things to settle usually misses the rebound. The most recent best day, 9 April 2025, came three trading days after a 6% fall.

Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections than lost in the corrections themselves.Peter Lynch

In fairness, the arithmetic cuts both ways. On paper, dodging the worst days helps as much as missing the best ones hurts. The catch is that doing one without the other means knowing in advance which day is which.

Missing the ten worst days would have turned $10,000 into $191,796. Nobody did that in practice, because those days were only obvious afterwards. Missing both the best and the worst, which is closer to what happens to someone who steps out in a fall and back in once it calms, ends near the $80,619 from simply staying put. All that effort, and twice the chance of getting a call wrong, for roughly the same place.

None of this needs a forecast. It is about exposure: a portfolio only collects the days it is invested for, and nobody knows in advance which days those will be. That is what "time in the market beats timing the market" means.

Closer to home

The FTSE 100 shows the same pattern. £10,000 invested at the end of 2012, with dividends reinvested, grew to £27,374 by the end of 2025. Missing its 10 best days left £17,906; missing 20 left £13,365. Five of its 10 best days came within a month of one of its 10 worst, and its best day of all, up 9.1% on 24 March 2020, was the same day the S&P 500 jumped 9.4%.

What this does not say. It does not say markets always recover quickly, or that a fall is a signal to buy. The figures cover one twenty-year stretch of the US market and thirteen years of the UK's, with dividends reinvested and before fees, taxes and currency moves; another period would give different numbers. Missed days are counted as earning nothing, so a strategy that held cash paying interest would do a little better. A broad market has historically recovered from its falls, though sometimes only after a long wait: Japan's main index took 34 years to regain its 1989 peak. A single company can fall and never come back.

Summary

01

A handful of days do most of the work.

Missing the 10 best of 5,029 trading days left $35,865 instead of $80,619 from $10,000 over twenty years.

02

The best days sit next to the worst.

Seven of the 10 best came within a month of one of the 10 worst, most of them in 2008 and 2020.

03

Selling in a crash usually means missing the rebound.

Dodging the worst days only pays with perfect foresight. Miss the best and worst together and you end up roughly where staying invested would have.

04

Time in the market beats timing it.

Staying invested is the only reliable way to be there for the days that matter.
