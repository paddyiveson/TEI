Why investors buy at the top

Rising prices feel like proof a decision was right, and that feeling tends to be strongest just when a price has moved furthest.

A queue outside a shop is not only a sign of demand. It can create demand of its own. A passer-by sees the line, takes it as evidence that something worthwhile is inside, and joins. The queue grows for reasons that have nothing to do with what is being sold.

Markets run on a similar mechanism. A rising price is visible: in an account balance, in a headline, in a friend's screenshot of gains. None of that confirms the business behind the price has improved. It only confirms the price has moved, and a moving price is what draws the next buyer in.

Bull markets are born on pessimism, grow on skepticism, mature on optimism and die on euphoria.Attributed to Sir John Templeton

The chart follows one cycle: how new buying grows as the price climbs, and what is pulling people in by the time it peaks.

Confidence and buying tend to rise with the price itself, not with new information about value. Each buyer's decision becomes evidence for the next one. It can look like many people confirming the same view independently. Often it is one signal, the price going up, read again and again.

So why does a careful, sensible person end up buying near the top? Rarely through carelessness. After a run of rising prices it is easy to credit good judgement rather than a rising market, so confidence is often highest when the room for disappointment is largest. Being wrong alongside everyone feels far safer than being wrong alone, so the crowd's view quietly takes the place of independent judgement. And the urge to stop watching others gain pulls people in late, usually after much of the rise has already happened.

None of these forces is foolish on its own. Confidence after success, trust in what others are doing and regret at missing out serve people well in plenty of everyday situations. The problem is timing: all three are most persuasive when the price has risen furthest, which is when the most is already assumed. Investors rarely buy at the top because they ignore the evidence. They buy there because the evidence they can feel has never been more convincing.

From our own portfolio

In late 2021, one of our team bought shares in a US maker of solar equipment. The story was everywhere: governments were backing clean energy, an energy shortage filled the headlines, and the investors we followed online called solar one of the defining trades of the decade. The shares had already risen about eightfold in under two years, and that climb read as proof the story was right, not as a warning. A year later they reached a new high, which felt like more proof. Then they fell for three years in a row, losing around half their value each year, and every fall looked like a chance to buy more at a better price. By late 2025 they were more than 90% below that high. It did not feel late at the time; it only looked late afterwards. The cost that never shows on a statement was the money tied up in one belief that could have been working somewhere else.

A past decision, shared to show the pattern above. It is not a view on any company today.

The question that cuts through a moment like that is whether anything about the business has changed, or only the price. It is the same gap that separates a good company from a good investment.

There is a measurable cost to this pattern, though buying near the top is only part of it. Studies of fund investors compare the return a fund earns with the return its investors actually receive, once the timing of every purchase and sale is counted. The difference is often called the behaviour gap.

The gap moves a lot from year to year. 2024 was one of the widest of the past decade, and in 2025 it nearly closed. Over longer stretches it persists, because buying after prices have risen is only half of it. The other half is selling after they fall, which is often just before the best days arrive, as missing the best days shows.

What this does not say. It does not say a rising price is a reason to sell, or that a fall is a reason to buy. The curve illustrates a pattern across crowds; it is not a chart of any market, and it cannot tell anyone where a price sits in its cycle today. Some rallies follow a real improvement in a business, and a price that looks stretched can keep rising for years. The gap figures are US data and vary widely: 8.5 points in 2024, under one point in 2025, and about half a point a year over ten years for investors in US stock funds. DALBAR and Morningstar measure the gap in different ways, and neither shows how any one investor did.

Summary

01

A rising price is only evidence that the price has risen.

Each buyer reads the move as confirmation, and their buying pushes the price on, drawing in the next.

02

Three forces peak together.

Certainty, the crowd and the fear of missing out are strongest when the price has moved furthest and the most is already assumed.

03

The cost shows up in returns.

Over the ten years to 2025, the average dollar in US funds earned about 1.2 points a year less than the funds themselves, because of when money went in and came out.
