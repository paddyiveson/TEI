What it is

Broad emerging market exposure in a single line.

XSOP is a passive exchange traded fund run by Xtrackers, the ETF arm of German asset manager DWS. It tracks the MSCI Emerging Markets Index, which covers large and mid-cap companies across roughly 24 developing economies, capturing around 85% of the free-float market value in each country.

Rather than picking individual emerging market stocks, XSOP buys a small slice of around 1,200 companies in one purchase. It is a passive, index-tracking fund, there is no manager trying to beat the market, just low-cost, broad access to it.

The fund is accumulating, which means dividends paid by the underlying companies are not sent to you. They are automatically reinvested inside the fund, which quietly increases its unit price over time rather than generating a cash payment. This makes it a pure growth-and-compounding holding rather than an income one.

Role in the portfolio

Why it sits in the Foundation sleeve.

The Growth sleeve of a TEI portfolio is concentrated and thematic, built around structural shifts the market has not yet fully priced. That is deliberate, but it also means the portfolio is not directly exposed to the broader growth story happening across the emerging world, the rise of a global middle class, the shift of manufacturing and technology production toward Asia, and economies growing considerably faster than developed markets.

XSOP is selected to close that gap in a single, low-cost holding. It gives the portfolio genuine geographic diversification beyond the UK and US, without requiring research into individual companies in markets that are harder to follow closely from the UK. It is not held because any single emerging market stock is a high-conviction idea. It is held because the portfolio should not be entirely absent from a third of the world's listed companies.

The mechanics, plainly

Buy roughly 1,200 companies across 24 emerging economies in one holding, weighted by company size within each country. Reviewed and rebalanced by MSCI on a quarterly basis. No stock-picking, no active management, dividends reinvested automatically rather than paid out.

Why the top 4 actually matter:

TSMC~13%

The world's dominant contract chipmaker, manufacturing the advanced semiconductors designed by Apple, NVIDIA and AMD. It is effectively the physical infrastructure behind the global AI boom. TSMC alone is held by 92% of global equity funds, more widely held than any US stock including Microsoft, and its index weight is now larger than the entire weighting of India.

Samsung Electronics~6%

South Korea's industrial giant, spanning memory chips, smartphones and consumer electronics. Up roughly 150% over the past year as AI-driven memory demand has surged. That scale of move has pushed the valuation to around 50 times cyclically adjusted earnings, a demanding price that leaves little room for disappointment.

SK Hynix~4%

A pure-play memory chip maker and direct beneficiary of AI data centre demand for high-bandwidth memory. Up roughly 200% in a year, and recently completed the largest ever US listing by a foreign company, raising $26.5 billion. Now trades at the most demanding valuation of the three chip names, around 125 times cyclically adjusted earnings.

Tencent~3%

China's internet and gaming giant, owner of WeChat. Represents the "old guard" of emerging market tech, and has lagged badly while the semiconductor names have soared, leaving it trading at a considerably cheaper valuation than its history. A potential value story if sentiment toward Chinese tech recovers, or a value trap if it does not.

Risk profile

What to actually understand before holding this.

  • Less diversified than it sounds. Taiwan, South Korea and China together make up roughly two-thirds of the fund, and just three semiconductor companies, TSMC, Samsung and SK Hynix, account for around 23% of the entire index between them. This is broad geographic exposure, but concentrated company and sector exposure.
  • Currency risk. Returns are affected by movements in a basket of emerging market currencies against sterling, on top of how the underlying shares perform.
  • Political and regulatory risk. Emerging markets can see abrupt policy changes, capital controls, or governance issues that are less common in developed markets, and China specifically carries geopolitical considerations that can move the fund meaningfully.
  • No income. Because the fund is accumulating, it is not suitable if the goal for this portion of the portfolio is cash income rather than growth.

The role, in one line: XSOP is not held for a single high-conviction reason. It is held so the portfolio has genuine, low-cost exposure to the emerging economies driving a meaningful share of global growth, compounding quietly in the background.
