How oil and bond yields affect equity markets

Oil reaches share prices through inflation and interest rates, and the same move does not always have the same effect.

Oil is not just a cost at the pump. It is an input cost for airlines, haulage firms and factories, and one of the numbers bond investors and central banks watch most closely for early signs of inflation. That is how a barrel of crude can end up changing the share price of a company that has nothing to do with oil.

The links do not move at the same speed. The real economy (links 2 to 4) takes weeks to months. Markets often jump straight from link 1 to link 5 within hours, because traders act on what they expect inflation and interest rates to do. Each link can break: a business may absorb a cost, or a central bank may hold rates.

Link 5 is where most explanations go wrong. A bond pays a fixed amount of interest, the coupon, set on the day it is issued. The yield is the return a buyer earns if they buy at today's market price and hold to the end. The coupon never changes. The price does, and the yield moves with it.

Nobody will pay £100 for a bond paying £5 a year when new bonds pay £6. Its price falls until a buyer collecting the remaining £5 payments, plus £100 at the end, earns the same 6%. Rising yields and falling bond prices are the same event, described two ways.

Higher yields do not hit every company equally. What matters is when its profits arrive: the further away a pound of profit is, the more a higher interest rate shrinks what it is worth today. The figure shows two companies with the same total profit, paid at different times.

A higher yield matters because it changes a comparison every investor makes: what can be earned safely, against what might be earned by taking risk. As the safe return climbs, shares have to offer a more convincing case. That is why fast-growing companies, expected to earn most of their profit years from now, tend to fall hardest when yields rise.

The chain is not just theory. In March 2026 it ran almost link by link: Brent crude rose 63% in a month, from $72.48 to $118.35 a barrel, the US 10-year government bond yield climbed from 3.96% to 4.44%, and the S&P 500 fell 7.8%.

Then it broke. From the end of March, shares climbed 23% to a record with oil still around $100, as strong company earnings outweighed the higher rate applied to future profits. By 27 October, Brent stood at $109.41 (from $60.75 in January), the US 10-year yield at 4.99% (from 4.19%) and the S&P 500 at 7,723 (from 6,858). Closer to home, the UK 10-year yield rose from 4.54% to 5.35%.

On 23 September yields jumped again after a report showed US business activity growing faster than expected: two causes travelling down the same chain at once. Markets react to the gap between the news and what was already expected, not to the news alone.

What this does not say. It does not forecast oil prices, interest rates or share prices. Each link in the chain can break: a business can absorb a cost, and a central bank can hold rates. The bond and the two companies are simplified illustrations, and 2026 is one stretch of one market.

Summary

01

Oil reaches shares through interest rates.

It feeds inflation and rate expectations first; the higher bond yields that follow change what investors pay for future profits.

02

Rising yields and falling bond prices are one event.

The coupon is fixed, so when the return investors demand goes up, only the price can adjust.

03

Far-off profits are hit hardest.

Companies expected to earn most of their profit years from now lose more value when rates rise.

04

Markets react to surprises.

What moves prices is the gap between what happened and what was already expected.
