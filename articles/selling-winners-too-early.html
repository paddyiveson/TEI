Why we sell winners too early

A rising share feels like something to protect, a falling one like something to deny. Neither feeling says anything about what happens next.

A share's purchase price works like a peg driven into the ground on the day it was bought. Every later price is measured against that peg, whether or not the peg has anything to do with where the price goes next. Psychologists call this anchoring.

A profit not yet sold is like the score at half-time: on the board, but nothing paid out. Selling is the final whistle. It turns a number on screen into money in the bank, or into a loss that cannot be undone.

Research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky found that losing money hurts roughly twice as much as gaining the same amount feels good. That imbalance pushes a rising position toward protection and a falling one toward denial.

Losses loom larger than gains.Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky

A study of 10,000 US brokerage accounts, covering trades between 1987 and 1993, measured this directly. On the days investors sold anything, they sold about 15% of the gains sitting in their accounts but only about 10% of the losses. Researchers call this the disposition effect.

The winners sold went on to beat the losers kept by 3.4 percentage points over the following year. That is one study, one market and one stretch of years: prices that have risen do not always keep rising, and a losing position is not owed a recovery.

1.5×The old market saying is: let your winners run and cut your losses short. These investors did the opposite. Shares that were up were sold 1.5 times as often as shares that were down.
Terrance Odean, "Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?", The Journal of Finance (1998): 10,000 US discount brokerage accounts, 162,948 trades, 1987 to 1993. On days an investor sold anything, they sold 14.8% of the shares showing a gain and 9.8% of those showing a loss, rounded here to 15 and 10 in every 100.

Palantir, a US data and software company, shows how hard this is to resist. An investor who put $10,000 in during the 2021 boom, at $36.05 a share, watched it shrink to $1,664 by the end of 2022, a fall of 83%. The chart follows that $10,000 through three decisions: holding on, selling once it doubled, or adding more during the fall.

Getting back to $10,000 took until September 2024. After a wait like that, every gain feels like something to bank before it disappears. Selling once the shares had doubled, in December 2024, turned the $10,000 into $21,176, and looked sensible: the valuation was stretched by any traditional measure. Holding on to 27 October 2026 would have left $55,126.

Some investors added during the fall instead. Putting in another $5,000 a year after buying, at $13.15, lowered the average price paid from $36.05 to $22.81, so the holding was back to break-even in February 2024, seven months sooner. It also put more money on one company: at the low, the $15,000 was worth $3,946.

Holding on, or adding, took conviction in the business, not comfort with the price, and that cuts both ways: plenty of fast growers fell as far in 2021 and 2022 and never came back, and adding to those made the loss bigger. One test investors use is to ask whether they would buy the shares today, at this price, if they did not already own them.

What this does not say. It does not say a share that has risen should be held, or that one that has fallen should be sold. The study covers one group of US investors from 1987 to 1993, and other periods could show a different gap. Palantir is one company that recovered; many that fell as far did not. Adding during a fall lowers the break-even price only by putting more money on the same company.

Summary

01

The price paid becomes a peg.

Later prices get measured against it instead of against what has changed in the business.

02

Gains get protected, losses get denied.

Investors in the study sold shares that were up 1.5 times as often as shares that were down.

03

Holding takes conviction in the business.

That conviction is only an edge when it rests on understanding, and it is costly when the understanding is wrong.
