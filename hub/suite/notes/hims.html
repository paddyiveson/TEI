Start Here: What Is Hims & Hers?

Hims & Hers Health (NYSE: HIMS) is a direct-to-consumer telehealth platform, founded in 2017, that connects patients with licensed clinicians online and ships prescription and over-the-counter treatments to their door. It built its early business on categories people found awkward to discuss in person - hair loss, erectile dysfunction, acne, anxiety and depression - marketed with bright, stigma-free branding. By mid-2026 it had grown to roughly 2.9 million subscribers and a run-rate above $3 billion in annual revenue, with the fastest-growing part of the business now weight-loss and GLP-1 medications rather than the original dermatology and sexual-health lines.

That pivot is also the source of nearly every controversy in this note. Hims built a meaningful share of its weight-loss revenue on compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide - pharmacist-mixed copies of Novo Nordisk's Wegovy/Ozempic and Eli Lilly's Zepbound/Mounjaro, legal only under specific FDA exemptions. As the underlying drug shortages that justified those exemptions were resolved through 2025 and 2026, the legal ground under that business narrowed, and Hims found itself in a public fight with the manufacturer whose drugs it was both partnering with and, in the manufacturer's view, imitating without permission.

In Plain English

What does "compounded" GLP-1 actually mean?

A compounding pharmacy can legally mix a custom version of an FDA-approved drug for an individual patient - for example, adjusting a dose or adding another ingredient for a documented medical reason. Federal rules also let compounders step in when a drug is in official shortage, making near-identical copies at scale. Semaglutide and tirzepatide were both on FDA shortage lists through 2023-2024, which is what allowed Hims, and dozens of smaller compounders, to sell cheaper "personalized" versions of Wegovy and Zepbound. Once the FDA declared both shortages resolved, that legal shortage exemption went away - compounding is still allowed for genuinely individualized prescriptions, but not as a way to mass-produce a copy of an approved drug just because it's cheaper.

Why This Matters For The Stock

A margin story hiding inside a growth story

Hims' headline numbers still look like classic hyper-growth: revenue up 38% year-over-year in Q2 2026, subscribers up 19%. But gross margin fell from 76% to 64% over the same year, and adjusted EBITDA margin was cut nearly in half (15% to 8%), because branded GLP-1 drugs purchased from Novo Nordisk cost far more than compounded ones did, and international expansion (via the Eucalyptus acquisition) carries a different cost structure. The stock's re-rating this year has largely been the market re-pricing that trade-off.

The Investment Thesis: A Real Platform, A Messier Weight-Loss Chapter

Hims' bull case rests on three things: a large, sticky, high-margin core telehealth business; a fast-growing (if lower-margin) weight-loss franchise now legitimized through a branded Novo Nordisk partnership; and a subscriber base and marketing engine that is genuinely hard to replicate. None of the three is free of real questions.

1. The core telehealth business is the quieter, more durable engine

Dermatology, sexual health, mental health and primary care - the categories Hims was built on - generated roughly 82% of total revenue as recently as 2024 and remain the majority of its subscriber base today, though the company has stopped disclosing an exact current split (flagged below). This is a high-margin, low-drama subscription business with a decade of operating history, and it is the part of the story least affected by the GLP-1 controversy.

2. Weight loss is now the growth engine - and the margin drag

Weight-loss revenue was projected at roughly $725 million for 2025 on its own, and management called it the fastest-growing segment heading into 2026, with testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) - a newer, related category - scaling toward a $100 million annual run rate. The March 2026 Novo Nordisk settlement gives Hims legal, branded access to Ozempic and Wegovy (pills and injections) at cash-pay prices, which management is framing as making Hims "the largest global consumer health platform" for affordable GLP-1 access. The cost is margin: branded drugs are more expensive inputs than compounded ones, which is the direct cause of this year's gross-margin compression.

3. Subscriber growth and international expansion are real, but partly acquisition-driven

International revenue grew over 1,600% year-over-year in Q2 2026, but almost entirely because of the Eucalyptus acquisition (an Australian telehealth platform) rather than organic international growth - a distinction worth keeping in mind when reading the headline growth rate. Underlying US revenue growth was a more modest 16% in the same quarter.

The Novo Nordisk Saga: Partnership, Public Breakup, Settlement

This is the single most important story in the Hims thesis, and it unfolded in full public view over roughly eleven months. It matters not just for what happened, but for what it reveals about how a manufacturer will respond when a distribution partner's marketing and a manufacturer's legal position collide.

Apr 2025

Novo Nordisk partners with Hims, LifeMD and Ro

Novo Nordisk's NovoCare Pharmacy struck simultaneous deals with three telehealth platforms to sell branded Wegovy at discounted cash prices - Hims at $599/month (all doses bundled with clinical support), LifeMD and Ro at $499/month. Hims stock jumped roughly 23% on the day; LifeMD jumped roughly 40%.

Jun 2025

Novo Nordisk terminates the Hims partnership - and sues

About a month later, Novo Nordisk pulled the deal specifically from Hims, publicly accusing the company of "deceptive promotion and selling of illegitimate, knockoff versions of Wegovy" and mass-producing compounded semaglutide in ways that violated compounding law, including allegations that some active ingredient was sourced from unauthorized Chinese manufacturers never inspected by the FDA. Novo also filed a separate patent-infringement suit. Hims shares fell as much as 35% on the news. LifeMD and Ro's separate arrangements were not reported to have been affected in the same way.

Feb 2026

Hims defends its position on the Q4 2025 earnings call

CEO Andrew Dudum told investors the weight-loss business was "durable" even in a "draconian scenario" where compounded GLP-1s went away entirely, pointing to broader weight-loss offerings and other categories to absorb the impact. FY2025 revenue came in at $2.35 billion, up from $1.5 billion in 2024, though net income was roughly flat at $128 million despite the revenue growth - an early sign of the margin pressure that would show up more sharply later.

Early 2026

Hims announces a "strategic shift" away from compounding

Ahead of the settlement, Hims said it would stop advertising compounded GLP-1 offerings altogether and transition existing patients to FDA-approved branded alternatives where clinically appropriate, keeping compounded semaglutide available only on a limited basis for patients whose needs genuinely cannot be met by an approved product.

Mar 9, 2026

Novo Nordisk and Hims settle

Novo dropped its patent-infringement suit (while explicitly reserving the right to refile) and the two companies struck a new commercial deal: Hims agreed to stop advertising compounded GLP-1 products, and in exchange gained the ability to sell branded Wegovy/Ozempic injections and pills at cash-pay prices, mirroring the arrangements Novo has with other telehealth partners. Shares rose on the news.

What's genuinely unclear: the settlement includes a carve-out letting Hims continue dispensing compounded GLP-1s "if a provider determines that a compounded product is clinically necessary" - language that is not precisely quantified anywhere in public reporting. How much of Hims' weight-loss revenue still runs through that carve-out versus branded product, and how durable Novo's truce is given it explicitly reserved the right to refile its patent suit, are not things this research could confirm with confidence. Treat the "settled" status as provisional rather than final.

The Regulatory Backdrop: Tighter Compounding Rules, a Live FTC Suit

FDA compounding rules have genuinely narrowed

As of the FDA's most recent clarification (spring 2026), both semaglutide and tirzepatide are officially off the drug shortage list and off the list of bulk substances that outsourcing facilities (503B) may use - meaning large-scale, factory-style compounding of GLP-1 copies is no longer legally available through that channel. Individual-pharmacy compounding (503A) for a specific patient with a valid, documented reason remains legal, and the FDA has said it will not enforce against pharmacies filling four or fewer prescriptions of a given compounded product per month - but a compounded product cannot be an "essentially identical copy" of an approved drug without a prescriber documenting a genuine clinical reason for the patient. This is a materially tighter environment than the one Hims built its 2023-2025 compounding growth on, and it is the regulatory backdrop the Novo settlement's "clinically necessary" carve-out has to operate inside.

A live FTC and multistate lawsuit, filed weeks before this note

On July 29, 2026, the FTC - joined by Utah and Los Angeles County on behalf of California - sued Hims & Hers in the Northern District of California, alleging violations of the FTC Act and the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA). The complaint alleges Hims charged patients immediately upon submitting an intake form, before any actual provider consultation, despite marketing that implied a consult would happen first; made cancelling a subscription deliberately difficult, including burying the cancel button behind several steps after a 2023 site redesign; and shared sensitive health information with third-party ad platforms including Meta and Snap in ways that contradicted the company's own privacy statements. Shares fell roughly 10% on the news. The case is pending, no penalty figure has been set, and Hims booked part of its $81 million in Q2 2026 one-time charges toward litigation accrual related to it.

Two distinct legal threads - the Novo Nordisk patent/compounding dispute and the FTC billing/privacy suit - are unresolved at the same time. Neither is a formal FDA enforcement action against Hims specifically, but both add real uncertainty to near-term cash flow (via legal costs and potential penalties) and to how much of the weight-loss compounding revenue is sustainable.

The bigger long-term threat may not be another telehealth platform at all. Both Eli Lilly (LillyDirect) and Novo Nordisk (NovoCare Pharmacy) now run their own direct-to-consumer channels selling self-pay GLP-1s straight to patients, cutting out telehealth intermediaries entirely for the price-sensitive segment of the market. Hims' pitch to patients is convenience, bundled clinical support, and a broader menu of conditions in one subscription - but if manufacturers keep investing in their own DTC experience and undercut telehealth partners on price, the "access layer" Hims occupies becomes easier to disintermediate over time. This is a structural risk to the weight-loss segment's long-run margin and growth, independent of the Novo/FTC disputes.

Near-Term Catalysts

Q2 2026 earnings (Aug 2026): Revenue beat ($753.2M, +38% YoY) alongside a GAAP net loss and clear margin compression - the market's read on this print has been mixed.

FTC/multistate lawsuit developments: Any motion to dismiss ruling, settlement talk, or penalty disclosure in the N.D. Cal. case would be the single biggest near-term legal catalyst.

Q3 2026 earnings (guided $880-900M revenue, $75-95M Adj. EBITDA): First full quarter to show whether gross margin stabilizes post-restructuring or keeps compressing.

Further FDA compounding guidance: Any additional narrowing (or clarification) of the 503A "clinically necessary" carve-out would directly affect how much of the weight-loss book Hims can legally retain outside the branded Novo relationship.

Analyst re-rating: Currently 1 strong buy, 2 buy, 11 hold, 1 sell across 15 analysts (avg PT $30.85, below the current price) - a shift in that mix would be a signal worth tracking.

The FTC case is a real legal and reputational overhang, not a formality. Allegations of charging before consultation, obstructing cancellation, and sharing health data with ad platforms go to the core of consumer trust in a healthcare subscription business. An adverse outcome could mean financial penalties, mandated product changes to the billing/cancellation flow, and further reputational damage beyond the roughly 10% single-day stock decline already seen on the filing.

The Novo Nordisk settlement explicitly is not final. Novo reserved the right to refile its patent-infringement claim, and the "clinically necessary" compounding carve-out is not precisely bounded in public disclosures. A renewed dispute, or aggressive interpretation of what counts as "clinically necessary" compounding by regulators, could reopen the same conflict that cost the stock roughly 35% in a single day in June 2025.

Margin trajectory is the key swing factor for the stock, not revenue growth. Revenue growth has stayed strong throughout this entire saga; profitability has not. Whether gross margin stabilizes in the mid-60s% or keeps compressing as branded-drug mix rises further will likely matter more to the share price over the next few quarters than the headline growth rate.

Manufacturers are building their own direct channels. LillyDirect and NovoCare Pharmacy both compete for the same self-pay GLP-1 patient Hims is chasing, and neither needs Hims as an intermediary. This is a slower-moving, structural risk rather than an event risk, but it caps how much pricing power Hims can ultimately hold in weight loss.

Insider selling adds a modest overhang. Public filings show approximately $58.5 million in insider sales over the trailing year, including a recent CFO Form 4 sale of 55,000 shares under a trading plan - not necessarily bearish on its own (trading plans are common), but worth tracking alongside the stock's already-elevated volatility (beta ~2.4).

This is a stock where the legal and regulatory calendar matters as much as the earnings calendar. Position sizing should account for event risk around FTC case developments and any further compounding-rule guidance, not just quarterly print volatility.

What would move this from watchlist to portfolio consideration: (1) a resolved or dismissed FTC case with a bounded, quantified outcome rather than an open-ended one; (2) two consecutive quarters of stable-to-improving gross margin, showing the branded-drug mix shift has run its course; (3) explicit company disclosure of the current weight-loss/GLP-1 revenue split, replacing the estimate-only picture available today; (4) an analyst re-rating that moves the consensus meaningfully off Hold with price targets converging above the current share price. Without at least one of these, the current risk/reward does not justify adding it to the fund over existing holdings.

Bottom Line: The Core Business Works. The Weight-Loss Chapter Is Still Being Written.

Hims & Hers has a genuinely durable, high-margin core telehealth business and a weight-loss franchise that, on revenue alone, is one of the fastest-growing consumer healthcare stories in the market. But 2025 and 2026 have shown, in vivid and repeated fashion, how much execution risk sits on top of that growth: a manufacturer partnership that blew up publicly within a month, a patent suit, a compounding-practice reckoning forced by the FDA's own shortage rules changing under the company's feet, and now an active FTC suit over how the subscription itself is billed and cancelled.

Near term: Q3 2026 earnings and any FTC case development are the two things most likely to move the stock materially over the next two quarters. Margin trajectory - not revenue growth, which has stayed strong throughout - is the number to watch.

Long term: if Hims stabilizes gross margin in the branded-GLP-1 era, resolves the FTC matter without a business-model-altering penalty, and successfully replicates its subscriber-acquisition playbook in TRT and other new categories, it has a credible path to being a durable, diversified consumer-health platform rather than a single-controversy stock. If margin keeps compressing or the legal overhangs escalate, the bull case narrows quickly.

TEI positioning: Watchlist - not a current Growth Fund holding. If added, this would sit at Tail tier within the Growth Fund sleeve - the core telehealth business is genuinely attractive, but GAAP unprofitability, an active FTC lawsuit with no bounded outcome yet, a provisional (not final) truce with a key drug supplier, and unresolved margin compression are too much simultaneous, un-quantified risk to justify a Core or Anchor-sized position today. TEI capital is currently better deployed in higher-conviction ideas already held in the fund - this is a capital-allocation call, not a rejection of the thesis. Monitor: Q3 2026 earnings, any FTC case ruling or settlement, and further FDA compounding guidance - whichever comes first.
