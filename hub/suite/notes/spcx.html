Start Here: What Is SpaceX Now?

SpaceX (formally Space Exploration Technologies Corp.) was founded in 2002 by Elon Musk to reduce the cost of spaceflight and eventually enable human settlement of Mars. In February 2026, SpaceX absorbed Musk's AI company xAI in an all-stock deal that valued the combined entity at roughly $1.25 trillion. Four months later, on June 11, 2026, the combined company priced the largest IPO in history — $135 a share, a $1.77 trillion valuation — and began trading on Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX. Shares briefly touched roughly $2.1 trillion in value on day one before settling into a volatile range that has since included a post-earnings surge, a new all-time low, and a $100 billion insider share unlock.

What actually trades under SPCX today is three businesses bolted together: the launch business (Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, Starship), the satellite-internet business (Starlink), and, new as of this year, an AI infrastructure and model business built around the former xAI (Grok, Colossus data centers, cloud compute sold to enterprise customers). Only one of the three — Starlink — is currently profitable.

In Plain English

Why would a rocket company buy an AI company?

Musk's stated logic is that AI compute needs enormous amounts of power and land, and SpaceX/Starlink already operate satellite ground infrastructure, energy relationships, and capital markets access at a scale few companies can match. Skeptics see something more prosaic: xAI was burning cash and needed a bigger balance sheet, and SpaceX's IPO gave Musk a vehicle to combine two of his companies' capital needs into a single, harder-to-scrutinize public raise. Both things can be true at once, and the market has not yet decided which explanation dominates — Q2 2026 results show the AI segment losing more money per quarter than the entire rest of the company earns.

Starship: Real Progress, Still Short of the Milestones That Matter

Starship is the vehicle SpaceX's longest-term ambitions — NASA's Artemis lunar program and any eventual Mars mission — depend on, and 2026 has been its most consequential year yet. Flight 12 (May 2026) was a partial success with a booster failure; Flight 13 (late July 2026) was a genuine milestone — nicknamed "Lucky 13," it achieved what multiple outlets called the softest ocean splashdown of the program to date, with the heat shield surviving reentry largely intact (though some independent observers flagged minor anomalies worth watching). Musk has said he now considers "the heat shield problem solved." Flight 14, targeted for before the end of August 2026 pending regulatory approval, is set to attempt the first deployment of operational payloads (newer Starlink V3 satellites) into orbit and the first tower ("chopstick") catch of the upper stage — both firsts for the program.

What's still missing: Orbital propellant transfer — refueling one Starship from another in space — has not yet been demonstrated. It is the single largest unresolved technical milestone standing between Starship and both a crewed Moon landing and any Mars-capable mission, since Starship's Moon/Mars payload capacity depends on multiple refueling flights per mission.

Artemis III (NASA) — slipping again

NASA officially still targets 2027 for the crewed Artemis III lunar landing, but that date is widely viewed as unrealistic. SpaceX's own internal estimates reportedly put the earliest crewed landing attempt at September 2028, a timeline the company has acknowledged falls outside its original NASA Human Landing System (HLS) contract. SpaceX and NASA are expected to negotiate revised milestones later in 2026. A renegotiated, still-unmet HLS timeline is now the base case, not a risk case.

Mars — a decade of slipping deadlines continues

Musk has targeted an uncrewed Starship Mars mission in the current Nov/Dec 2026 Earth-Mars transfer window, with earlier public odds framed around "50-50." The most recent reporting available at the time of writing suggests this window's uncrewed attempt is being scaled back or scrubbed again — consistent with a pattern stretching back to 2016, when SpaceX first targeted an uncrewed Mars landing for 2018 (later moved to 2022, then 2026, and now likely 2028 at the earliest). We flag this explicitly as unresolved and rapidly moving — treat any specific Mars date from SpaceX as aspirational rather than committed.

Falcon 9 Cadence and the Government/Defense Book

While Starship and AI dominate headlines, the workhorse Falcon 9/Falcon Heavy fleet keeps compounding. SpaceX reported 78 orbital missions and roughly 1,041 metric tons delivered to orbit in Q2 2026 alone — a cadence no competitor is close to matching, built on Falcon 9's now-routine booster reuse. That scale is also the foundation of SpaceX's government relationship: SpaceX won the majority of U.S. national security launches awarded under the Space Force's NSSL Phase 3 program for fiscal year 2026, and Lane 1 (the more flexible, commercial-style contract lane) had its capacity tripled to roughly $17 billion in July 2026 as the Pentagon leaned further into SpaceX-style rapid, reusable launch.

SpaceX has also become a direct contractor on the Trump administration's "Golden Dome" missile-defense initiative, reportedly winning a $2.29 billion Space Force contract for a Golden Dome data-relay/tracking backbone, plus additional space-layer satellite awards that press reports have put in a $4-6.5 billion range (exact scope and whether these figures overlap is not fully clear from public reporting). Combined with existing Starshield contracts — SpaceX's classified/military variant of Starlink, worth $6 billion-plus in multi-year government awards — defense and national-security work is now a meaningful, sticky revenue stream layered on top of Starlink and commercial launch.

This defense concentration cuts both ways for a watchlist thesis: it is high-margin, multi-year, hard-to-displace revenue, but it also means a growing share of SpaceX's business is tied to U.S. defense budget cycles and a specific administration's missile-defense priorities — a political and budgetary dependency that didn't exist for SpaceX a few years ago.

The bull case for the current multiple rests on Musk's public projection that AI revenue could reach $300-500 billion annually by the end of 2027 at full data-center build-out — a figure management has stated but not yet substantiated with more than one quarter of results. The bear case is simpler: SpaceX is now spending like an AI hyperscaler ($15.8 billion of quarterly AI capex alone) while carrying the capital intensity of a rocket company, and Wall Street's open question — "how many years can you lose $15B+ a quarter on AI infrastructure" — is unresolved. TEI readers who hold Nebius will recognize this pattern: heavy AI-infrastructure capex ahead of proven demand is exactly the dynamic driving Nebius's own volatility in the fund today, just at roughly 100x the balance-sheet scale.

Key Risks

One quarter of public track record $15.8B/qtr AI capex vs. ~$1.2B FCF ~21x forward P/S Lock-up overhang through Dec '26 Starship/HLS delay pattern Mars timeline repeatedly slipped Musk attention split across 4+ companies

This is a two-and-a-half-month-old public stock trading at a mega-cap valuation. Every public-market metric — price history, analyst estimate accuracy, insider-selling patterns, post-lock-up trading behavior — has essentially one data point behind it. Extrapolating a durable trend from one earnings report is a genuine methodological problem, not a formality.

The AI segment is the newest, least-proven, and most capital-hungry part of the company. It lost more money in Q2 ($1.26B operating loss) than the entire rest of the company earned, while consuming $15.8 billion of that quarter's capex. If AI infrastructure demand or pricing disappoints, this segment could impair the (currently strong) Starlink-funded balance sheet rather than the reverse.

Starlink's growth is real but its unit economics are softening. ARPU fell from roughly $85 to $66 per month year-over-year as the subscriber base scales into lower-revenue international, enterprise-bulk, and direct-to-cell segments. Continued subscriber growth at declining ARPU is a different, less favorable story than growth at stable pricing.

Starship and Artemis III have a documented pattern of missed dates. NASA's own safety panel and multiple independent reports have flagged Starship HLS delays for years; the company itself now says its internal timeline falls outside its original NASA contract. Treat any specific Starship or Mars date from SpaceX as directional, not committed — this has been true for a decade and nothing in 2026's results changes that pattern.

Governance and key-person concentration are unusually high even for a founder-led company. Elon Musk simultaneously runs Tesla, X, Neuralink, and now a combined SpaceX/xAI that just became a $1.5 trillion public company — with a documented history of aggressive public timelines that don't hold. The lock-up unlock schedule (staggered through roughly December 2026) also means a meaningful share of insider liquidity events are still ahead, not behind.

This is not the "can't buy it, so it's just context" situation this note was originally framed around. SPCX is a real, ownable Nasdaq ticker today. The risk case here is closer to "extremely early, extremely expensive, extremely levered to an AI capex bet layered on top of an already-ambitious rocket program" — a different, but not smaller, set of reasons for caution.

What would move this from watchlist to portfolio consideration: (1) at least two to three more quarters of public results showing the AI segment's losses narrowing rather than widening; (2) the remaining 2026 lock-up tranches (through roughly December 8) absorbed without a sustained valuation air-pocket, similar to how August 6 traded; (3) a NASA-agreed, credible revised Artemis III/HLS date that SpaceX is visibly tracking to, rather than another unacknowledged slip; (4) the forward P/S multiple compressing meaningfully from ~21x as the business's earnings catch up to its valuation. Without at least two of these, the current risk/reward does not justify adding SPCX to the fund over existing holdings — regardless of how strong the long-run Starship/Starlink thesis is.

Bottom Line: Investable Now, But Too New to Trust Yet

SpaceX enters this batch of notes as a genuine outlier — not because it can't be owned, but because almost everything about its public existence is brand new. Ten weeks ago it didn't have a ticker. Two and a half months ago it wasn't an AI company. The underlying operating businesses — Falcon 9's launch dominance, Starlink's 12 million subscribers and real profitability, Starship's genuine (if repeatedly delayed) technical progress, a defense book that keeps growing — are as strong as any thesis in this batch. What's missing is a public track record long enough to know whether the AI segment is a smart diversification funded by Starlink's cash flow, or a capital sink that eventually strains the parts of the business that actually work.

Near term: Starship Flight 14 (payload deployment + tower catch attempt), the remaining 2026 lock-up tranches, and the first Artemis III timeline renegotiation are the three things worth tracking most closely over the next two quarters.

Long term: if Starlink's economics hold as ARPU normalizes, the AI segment reaches a credible path to profitability, and Starship clears orbital refueling, SPCX could justify its premium multiple as a genuinely unique combination of infrastructure businesses. If the AI build-out disappoints while Starship keeps slipping, the multiple has a long way to compress.

TEI positioning: Watchlist - not a current Growth Fund holding. Monitor as ecosystem context, not a near-term fund candidate: SPCX is technically investable, which is new, but one quarter of results and an unfinished lock-up schedule are not enough evidence to size a position against holdings like Nebius or ASTS that have multi-year track records. If added down the line, this would sit at Tail tier within the Growth sleeve given the position-sizing discipline a two-month-old, ~$1.5 trillion, still-unlocking listing demands — not Core or Anchor, regardless of how large or dominant the underlying businesses are. TEI capital is currently better deployed in higher-conviction ideas already held in the fund - this is a capital-allocation call, not a rejection of the thesis. Monitor: Starship Flight 14 results, the December 2026 lock-up completion, and Q3 2026 earnings (expected ~November 2026), whichever clarifies the AI-segment trajectory first.
