Start Here: Why an Obscure German Laser Company Matters for AI

LPKF Laser & Electronics SE is a 50-year-old German company that makes laser equipment. Most of its revenue comes from products you have probably never thought about: circuit board prototyping systems for university labs, laser welding systems for car parts, and scribers for solar panels. None of that is particularly exciting. What has made LPKF one of the most talked-about small-cap stocks in the AI semiconductor supply chain is a single patented process called LIDE — and the role it plays in the next generation of chip packaging.

To understand why that matters, you need to understand a problem that is quietly causing headaches at the highest levels of the semiconductor industry: AI chips are getting too big and too powerful for the packaging materials that currently hold them together.

The Problem

AI Chips Are Outgrowing Their Packaging

A semiconductor chip is not just a bare piece of silicon. It sits inside a package — a structured housing that provides electrical connections to the outside world, manages heat, and physically protects the chip. The substrate is the base layer of that package: a flat material that the chip sits on, with fine wiring embedded inside it to route electrical signals between the chip and the circuit board below.

For decades, substrates have been made from organic materials — essentially high-performance fibreglass, similar in concept to the epoxy-resin composite used in printed circuit boards. Organic substrates have worked well for smaller chips, but as AI accelerators have grown dramatically larger and more powerful, organic materials are reaching their physical limits in three specific ways.

First, warpage: organic substrates expand and contract at a rate about seven times faster than silicon when heated. A large AI chip generates significant heat. As the chip expands and the substrate tries to keep up, the package bends and warps — cracking solder joints and destroying connections. NVIDIA's Blackwell B200 chip is already so large that two dies had to be placed side by side in a single package, and the next-generation Rubin GPU will be even larger. At these scales, organic substrate warpage is not a minor inconvenience; it is an engineering crisis.

Second, signal loss: organic substrates have relatively high dielectric loss — meaning that high-frequency electrical signals degrade as they travel through the material. As chips run faster and communicate at higher data rates, this signal degradation becomes a fundamental performance ceiling.

Third, wiring density: packing more connections into a smaller space requires finer and finer wiring traces. Organic materials can only be manufactured to a certain level of precision before yields collapse.

Think of it this way: Organic substrates are like a road network built for horse-drawn carriages. For decades they were perfectly adequate. But AI chips are the equivalent of Formula 1 cars — and the road simply cannot handle the speed, the load, or the precision required. The semiconductor industry needs to rebuild the roads. Glass is the new asphalt.

The Solution

Why Glass Is the Next Substrate Material

Glass has properties that make it almost perfectly suited to replace organic substrates in advanced packaging. Its thermal expansion rate closely matches silicon, virtually eliminating warpage. Its dielectric properties are significantly better than organic materials, reducing signal loss at high frequencies. It can be manufactured at extraordinary flatness — surface roughness in the single-nanometre range, orders of magnitude better than organic. And it can be processed on large panels (510x515mm or larger), covering three to four times the area of a 300mm silicon wafer, making it highly cost-efficient at volume.

Intel publicly committed to glass core substrates in 2023 and shipped the world's first commercial processor using a glass core substrate in January 2026 (the Xeon 6). TSMC has its CoPoS (Chip-on-Panel-on-Substrate) platform in pilot production, targeting mass production in 2028. Samsung Electro-Mechanics is in joint development. SKC's Absolics subsidiary in Georgia, USA, is preparing for commercial production. The transition from organic to glass in advanced AI chip packaging is not speculative — it is underway.

Think of it this way: The semiconductor industry switching from organic to glass substrates is the equivalent of switching from wooden railway sleepers to concrete. The train still runs on the same tracks and carries the same cargo — but the foundation is fundamentally more stable, more durable, and capable of handling much higher speeds and loads. The transition takes time, but once it begins at scale, there is no going back.

The Chokepoint

What Is a Through-Glass Via, and Why Is It So Hard to Make?

Glass is an excellent substrate material, but it has one fundamental challenge: you cannot route electrical signals through a sheet of solid glass. To make glass useful as a packaging substrate, you need to create thousands of extremely precise vertical holes through it — called Through-Glass Vias (TGVs) — which are then filled with copper so that electrical signals can pass from one side to the other.

Drilling TGVs sounds straightforward. In practice, it is extraordinarily difficult. Glass is brittle. A standard laser creates heat and stress that cracks or chips the glass around the hole, leaving debris and rough edges that prevent reliable copper filling. A mechanical drill creates similar damage at the microscale. The tolerances required are extraordinary: holes as small as 6 micrometres in diameter (a human hair is 70 micrometres), with walls vertical to within fractions of a degree, no cracking, no debris, at production speed, across panels containing millions of vias.

This is precisely the problem that LPKF's LIDE technology solves.

Think of it this way: Imagine drilling thousands of perfectly vertical, perfectly smooth holes through an extremely thin pane of fine crystal glass, each hole narrower than a human hair, without cracking the glass, leaving any debris, or creating any roughness that would prevent them from being reliably filled. With standard tools, this is essentially impossible to do at the precision and speed required. LIDE makes it possible. That is why the process matters.

LPKF's Technology

LIDE: Laser-Induced Deep Etching

LIDE is LPKF's patented two-step process for creating Through-Glass Vias. Step one: a femtosecond laser (a laser that fires in pulses of millionths of a billionth of a second) passes through the glass, creating a precise structural modification track along the intended via path without generating heat or cracking the glass. The laser changes the glass structure at a microscopic level without removing material. Step two: a controlled chemical etch selectively removes the modified glass along the laser track, creating a clean, smooth, perfectly formed via with no debris, no cracks, and walls that are straight enough for reliable copper filling.

The result is a TGV production process that can create millions of vias per panel with the precision, yield, and throughput that commercial semiconductor packaging requires. LPKF has expanded the LIDE technology into a family of products — the NEXAR tool family includes the LIDE 5000 for via formation, the Ablate 5000 for glass ablation and scribing, and the Bond 5000 for multilayer glass bonding. The company is also developing "Tensor," a panel-level glass processing platform for high-volume production.

LPKF states that approximately 80% of the world's major customers making equipment decisions in glass substrate advanced packaging have chosen LPKF equipment. This figure, repeatedly cited by management and referenced by Serenity in his thesis, positions LIDE as the dominant TGV process in the industry today — with competing approaches from SCHMID and TRUMPF, and emerging Chinese domestic players, as yet unable to match LIDE's precision and yield at scale.

The critical insight: LPKF does not make glass. It does not make chips. It makes the equipment that converts raw glass panels into usable glass substrates — specifically the laser system that creates the microscopic vias that make glass electrically functional. If LPKF's 80% market share claim is accurate, then nearly every glass substrate being qualified or produced today was drilled with LPKF equipment. That is a structural position remarkably similar to how ASML sits in EUV lithography: not the most visible company in the supply chain, but potentially the one you cannot get around.

The Company Today: Four Segments, One Exciting Thesis

LPKF is not purely a glass substrate play. It is a diversified laser equipment company that happens to own the most advanced TGV drilling technology in the world. Understanding the full business is essential because the financial picture today looks very different from the thesis picture in 2027-2028.

Laser scribers for thin-film solar cell manufacturing and digital paste printing. This segment is the primary reason FY2026 guidance shows negative EBIT. The key tension in the LPKF investment case: The market is pricing LPKF as a glass substrate pure-play. The actual business generates the majority of current revenue from PCB prototyping, plastic welding, and solar equipment — none of which have anything to do with the AI packaging thesis. The glass substrate LIDE thesis is real but it is essentially a free option embedded inside a currently loss-making industrial equipment company. Pricing that option correctly is the core analytical challenge.

The Market Opportunity: From Near-Zero to Potentially $8B+

The glass substrate market is very early stage, which is both the opportunity and the risk. Current revenues from glass substrates are a small fraction of LPKF's total. But the trajectory of the market — driven by Intel, TSMC, Samsung, and Absolics all racing to qualify glass for AI chip packaging — is one of the most clearly defined growth curves in the semiconductor equipment sector.

The market size estimates vary enormously depending on how broadly you define "glass substrate" packaging. The narrow definition (glass core substrates for chip packaging) has the market at $460-586M by 2030. Broader definitions that include panel-level glass interposers, glass carriers, and glass-integrated CPO get to $8.1B by 2030 (Counterpoint Research) and $14.7B by 2030 on the most expansive view. The equipment market (where LPKF sits) is a fraction of the substrate market itself — typically 10-20% of material cost — but at the volume ramp phase, equipment must be purchased ahead of production, creating a concentrated demand event.

LPKF management's explicit disclaimer: The company has stated clearly that "potential high-volume orders from the Advanced Semicon Packaging area are not included in this forecast, as the timing of the market-expected ramp-up is determined not by the LIDE process provided by LPKF, but by the qualification of downstream process steps." This is unusually honest guidance. LPKF is telling investors: we cannot control when Absolics, Samsung, and TSMC qualify their downstream processes. When they do, we believe the orders will come to us. But we will not put that in our guidance until we have signed contracts. This is credible management behaviour — and it means that when volume orders do arrive, they will be a genuine positive surprise to reported guidance.

The gap is almost comically large. ASML — the closest structural analogy, as the monopoly provider of the most critical equipment process in chip manufacturing — is valued at 400 times LPKF's market cap. The point of the comparison is directional, not predictive. If glass substrates become standard in AI chip packaging — and Intel, TSMC, Samsung, and every major substrate producer all say they will — and if LPKF's LIDE technology remains the dominant TGV process for the next 5-10 years, then the revenue trajectory and margin potential of LPKF in 2030 could look meaningfully different from 2026. The semicap giants were once small, specialist, pre-profitability equipment suppliers that happened to own a critical process. Their growth trajectories are the template for understanding what LPKF's ceiling might be.

The honest valuation context: At ~€750M market cap on €115M in current revenue, LPKF is already trading at approximately 6.5x revenue — roughly in line with established profitable semicap peers on a P/S basis, even though LPKF is loss-making. The market has essentially pre-priced a significant portion of the glass substrate upside based on the narrative. Whether that pre-pricing is correct depends almost entirely on the volume order timeline. The stock is not cheap on current fundamentals. It is priced as an option on the glass substrate ramp.

What the 2026 Guidance Actually Says

Management guides €105-120M revenue and an adjusted EBIT margin of -3% to +4.5% for FY2026. This implies an adjusted EBIT between approximately -€3.15M and +€5.4M. On top of this, restructuring costs under the North Star transformation programme are expected to add an additional 3-4% of revenue in 2026 — meaning reported EBIT will be materially worse than the adjusted figures. The company has a working capital headroom of €25M — adequate but not abundant if orders materialise faster than expected.

The critical phrase in management's guidance: "Potential volume orders from the Advanced Semicon Packaging area are not included in this forecast." This is the embedded upside that the market is pricing in but LPKF itself will not commit to. When those orders arrive and are included in guidance, the revenue trajectory changes fundamentally. Until then, the base business is declining and loss-making.

What Would Change the Financial Picture

A single volume production order from one major glass substrate maker (Absolics, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, or a TSMC supply chain partner) would represent a step-change in LPKF's order book. Each NEXAR LIDE 5000 production system is a high-value capital equipment order. A batch of 5-10 systems ordered for an initial production line would represent a material addition to the current €34M order backlog. If Serenity is correct and Absolics begins the ramp in H2 2026, that catalyst could arrive within the current financial year — outside current guidance.

Watchlist Verdict: The Thesis Is Real. The Timing Is the Question.

LPKF owns a genuinely important process. Glass substrates are not speculative — Intel has shipped a commercial product using one, TSMC is in pilot production, and every major AI chip maker has glass on their packaging roadmap. If even a fraction of that roadmap converts to production at volume, and if LPKF's 80% equipment share claim is validated at the production stage, the revenue and margin trajectory changes fundamentally from the current loss-making base business.

The structural comparison to ASML is deliberately provocative. ASML now generates €28 billion in revenue at 34% margins as the monopoly provider of EUV lithography. LPKF's LIDE is not EUV — glass substrates serve a narrower application set and face real competition. But the principle holds: a company that owns the dominant process for the hardest step in a manufacturing transition that the entire semiconductor industry is committed to executing can compound extraordinary value over 5-10 years. The question is whether LPKF is that company, or whether SCHMID or a Chinese domestic player takes share before volume orders arrive.

What would move this from watchlist to portfolio consideration: First volume production equipment order from a named customer (Absolics, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, or TSMC supply chain). A pullback in the stock price that improves the entry risk/reward to reflect current fundamentals rather than future potential. And H1 2026 results (July 23) showing Electronics segment order intake continuing to accelerate. Without at least one of these conditions being met, the current risk/reward does not justify adding to the fund over the existing holdings with stronger near-term fundamentals.
