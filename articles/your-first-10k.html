Why your first £10,000 is so difficult

The first £10,000 asks more of a saver than almost any stretch that follows it. Not because the number is large, but because almost none of the lifting is being done by anything except the saver.

A rocket burns most of its fuel just leaving the ground. Once it is moving, gravity fights it far less, and the same thrust carries it much further. The first £10,000 works the same way for an investment pot.

At this stage, growth has almost nothing to work with. A small pot earns a small amount, even at a healthy rate of return, so nearly every pound of progress has to come from the saver directly. Almost all of the thrust is coming from one engine.

Take the £200-a-month, 7% example used across this series. It takes 45 months, just under four years, to cross £10,000. Of that, £9,000 came directly from the saver, and only about £1,200 came from growth. Growth barely speeds the stretch up, either: with no growth at all, the same saving would get there in 50 months. A different contribution or return changes the exact split, but the pattern holds. This early, the saver is doing almost all of the work.

£200 paid in at the end of each month, growing at 7% a year compounded monthly, before fees, taxes and charges. An illustration, not a forecast.

This does not mean the first stretch is wasted, or that it should feel discouraging. It also does not stay this hard. In the same example, growth's share of the pot keeps climbing at every milestone after this one.

By £25,000, growth is about a quarter of the pot. By £50,000, it is closer to two fifths. By £100,000, after just under 20 years, growth is more than half of everything in the pot. Every milestone after the first one leans a little more on growth and a little less on the saver.

The stretch beyond that is a different story again: the next £100,000 arrives in less than half the time of the first. The first £10,000 is mostly a saving problem. Later milestones become a growth problem too. This is specific to this £200-a-month, 7% example; a different contribution or return moves every one of these numbers.

This is also why extra money matters most exactly when it feels least rewarding. A pound added during the hard early stretch has decades left to compound. The same pound added later does not.

Take the same 30 years of £200 a month, and add an extra £50 a month for four years: £2,400 in total. Added during the first four years, it leaves the pot about £16,000 larger by year 30. Added during the last four years, it adds only about £2,700, because it barely has time to compound. The pound is identical. The timing is not.

What this does not say. None of this says how long it should take any particular saver to reach £10,000, or what they should be contributing to get there. It shows why that first stretch asks more of a saver than the ones that follow it, and why money added during it tends to count for more than the same money added later. The lifting gets easier. It does not disappear.

Summary

01

The saver does the early lifting.

In the first stretch, growth has almost nothing to grow, so nearly every pound of progress comes from money put in.

02

Growth's share climbs with the pot.

The same 7% does very little on £1,000 and a great deal on £100,000, because it is a percentage of two very different numbers.

03

Early money is worth more.

A pound added in the first few years has the longest to compound, so it ends up worth several times the same pound added near the end.
