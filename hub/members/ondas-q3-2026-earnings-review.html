    2.1Selling more, keeping less

    How short selling works, and what "covering" means
    An example with 100 shares
    1 · Borrow100 sharesfrom a broker or fund, paying a fee while they are on loan
    2 · Sell nowAt $5 = $500The seller has cash but owes 100 shares back
    3 · CoverBuy 100 back"Covering" is buying the shares back to close the bet
    4 · ReturnHand them backThe loan ends
    If the price falls to $4+$100

    Buying back costs $400. The seller keeps the $100 difference, less fees.

    If the price rises to $7−$200

    Buying back costs $700. There is no ceiling on how high a price can go, so losses have no fixed limit.

    Why covering moves the priceA squeeze

    Every short seller must eventually buy. If the price rises and many cover at once, their buying pushes it higher still, which forces more to cover.

    Why days to cover matters~4 days

    Shares sold short divided by the shares that normally trade in a day. It shows how long the exit takes. The longer it is, the more buying is still to come if sellers rush to cover, and the harder a squeeze can push. About 4 days is crowded; above about 10 is usually seen as very crowded. It also rises when trading volume falls.

If margins recover as management expects, today's price looks low and the large short position could unwind quickly. If they do not, the sellers will have been right.

    Part of 10 · Our view

    What $5 is a bet on

    If you buy an Ondas share at $5, what has to go right for it to pay off, and what could it be worth by the end of 2028?

    It is a round number close to where Ondas has traded since its Q3 results ($4.39 to $5.73), and it keeps the sums easy to follow.

    $1 in 2028 is worth less than $1 today. You could have invested it elsewhere in the meantime, and with a loss-making company there is a real chance of getting less back.

    So the question is not just "will Ondas be worth more than $5?", but "will it be worth enough more to make the wait and the risk worthwhile?" We set that bar at 12% a year. A steadier company might need 8%; a riskier one, more.

    This bar is called the discount rate. We use it to bring values in 2028 back to today, so they can be compared with $5.

    Why Watch closely, not Reassess

    Q3 delivered the growth and missed on efficiency. That is what a company looks like straight after an intense run of acquisitions: several businesses being joined together, new production lines starting up, and costs arriving before the savings.

    If Ondas can iron out those inefficiencies and streamline, the business is already built to ramp very quickly. That makes this a serious opportunity with real execution risk, which is usually where the opportunity is.

    Growth is expected. Profit has to be earned.
