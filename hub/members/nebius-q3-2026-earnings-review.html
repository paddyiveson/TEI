New to Nebius? Think of it as a landlord for AI computing power

Nebius builds data centres full of Nvidia chips and rents that computing power to companies building AI. Its buildings are measured in megawatts (MW) of power, and its rent is the yearly contract value per megawatt. Income grows when more rooms are switched on, and when the rent per room goes up.

Our October note, "Nebius: Sizing up the Opportunity", sets out the full case and what we were looking for from this quarter. We check the results against it further down.

The rent has roughly doubled this year

Nebius started 2026 with a stated base of about $12m of yearly contract value per megawatt. Deals signed in Q2 came in at $20m to $25m. Q3 deals closed above $25m. Short contracts of up to six months, for customers who need capacity now, are being negotiated at $40m to $50m.

New ways of selling are helping set those prices: reserve auctions and live spot prices for spare capacity. Both auction winners were new customers, and one has since grown its contract tenfold. Demand is running well ahead of what Nebius can switch on.

Customers are paying for the build up front

On deals signed in Q3, prepayments covered more than 100% of the cost of building the capacity, including the data centre and networking. On Q2 deals it was 50% to 60%. More than 90% of Q3 contract value came with a prepayment, and Nebius says the trend has continued into Q4.

Those prepayments show up on the balance sheet as "deferred revenue", which has grown six-fold in nine months. What that is, and whether it is good news, is explained next.

Deferred revenue: good news, with two things to watch

Deferred revenue is money a customer has paid, or been billed, for a service Nebius has not delivered yet. Think of it as rent paid in advance. Until Nebius switches the computing power on and the customer uses it, Nebius owes that service, so the amount sits on the balance sheet as a liability, not as income. Each month the service is delivered, part of it moves across into revenue.

At Nebius it has grown six-fold in nine months, from $1.6bn to $9.4bn, and it now makes up about a third of everything Nebius owes. It is not borrowing. But it is a promise: the work still has to be done.

Why it is good

  • Customers have committed and paid, or been billed, in advance. That is strong evidence of demand.
  • The money helps pay for the build, so Nebius needs less from lenders and new shares.
  • Most of it is long term: $8.0bn of the $9.4bn is due to be earned more than a year from now.

What to watch

  • Billed is not the same as paid. Customers owed Nebius $4.0bn at 30 September, much of it for these prepayments. Until it is collected, it is not cash. We look at this under funding.
  • It only becomes revenue once the capacity is delivered. Delays like New Jersey push revenue later, and the obligation stays until the service is provided.

So it is both: strong evidence of demand, and a promise that has to be kept.

More than a landlord: Token Factory

Token Factory is Nebius's service for running AI models for customers, charged by use rather than by the chip. It is small today, approaching a run rate of several hundred million dollars less than a year after launch, but contract value grew 17 times in Q3.

It matters for the thesis because it earns more from the same megawatt. Nebius says it runs at better margins because it uses its own chips rather than rented ones. New AI models go live on it the day they are released. If it keeps growing, Nebius is not only renting out chips, and its income becomes less tied to the price of raw computing power.

Nebius does not report Token Factory's revenue or margin separately, so its size can be described but not yet measured.

  • Bigger contracts. Three months ago Nebius reported its first contracts worth over $1bn. In early Q4 it says it is already signing multi-billion dollar agreements.
  • New kinds of customer. Databricks, Datadog and Snowflake, three large enterprise data and AI platforms, signed multi-year agreements for the first time.

Underlying growth was about 13%

Nebius has started an "asset-light" model: partners pay for the capacity and run it on Nebius's design and software. The first deal produced about $325m of revenue in Q3, booked when the capacity and supply chain were locked in. More follows as that capacity comes online.

This is not necessarily a one-off. Nebius says it is building a pipeline of these partnerships and expects them to become a more regular part of the business, and its 2027 guide includes none of this revenue. But the payments will be lumpy, so they cloud the quarterly picture. Without this deal, revenue grew about 13% on the quarter, down from 46% in Q2, as fewer new rooms switched on than planned.

About two months, outside Nebius's control

The main delay is at Vineland in New Jersey, a site run by a landlord called DataOne and largely booked by Microsoft. Late in Q3, DataOne began switching off the site's temporary power generation ahead of the move to permanent power. That reset the schedule for the remaining capacity.

Permanent power is coming from Bloom Energy fuel cells, which are already running and being scaled up. Nebius expects more than half of the Microsoft capacity by the end of the year and the rest in Q2 2027. The contracts behind the delayed capacity remain in place.

The fix: own more of the sites

Nebius says all its own sites are on schedule. It is putting most of its next capacity on land it controls: Missouri, Finland, Alabama and, later, Pennsylvania. Owned sites mean more control over timing, and over time a building on the balance sheet rather than rent in the costs. The price is more spending now, which is why 2026 capex went up.

When does revenue catch up with spending?

Nebius plans to add more than 1 GW of capacity every year. At today's cost, each gigawatt takes $40bn or more, so capex stays at about $40bn to $45bn a year for as long as it keeps growing at that pace.

Revenue grows into that spending with a lag. On the middle of the estimates, it gets close to the capex line around 2029. Only when Nebius slows the build does capex fall away while the built sites keep earning. That is the moment the cash arrives.

That is the bet an investor makes on Nebius today: that it delivers the sites on time, at today's prices, and funds the gap until then. It is also why delays matter so much. Use the buttons to see how the picture changes.

Profit and cash are not the same thing

Capex does not hit profit straight away. A data centre's cost is spread over its life as depreciation, about five years for the computers. That is why Nebius can reach pre-tax breakeven in Q4 2027 while still spending $40bn or more a year.

Cash is different. While spending outruns the cash the business brings in, the gap has to be filled by prepayments, borrowing or new shares. So the profit date arrives first. The cash turning point comes later, when the build slows.

Is the 2027 guide too low?

A well-known short seller pointed out that $12bn to $13bn of revenue, spread over about 1,500 MW of average capacity in 2027, works out at only about $8m to $9m per megawatt. That is far below the $25m Nebius is signing new deals at.

It cuts both ways. Against: the higher prices are not showing in reported revenue yet. For: if the average price rises even part of the way, the guide looks easy to beat. Each extra $1m per megawatt on 1,500 MW adds about $1.5bn of revenue. The average will rise slowly, though. The Microsoft and Meta contracts are fixed for five years at lower prices, and new sites only earn for part of the year they switch on.

What the market is paying for

Nebius owns more than its cloud business. Its biggest other holding is a stake of about a quarter of ClickHouse, a fast-growing database company, and it owns most of Avride, which builds self-driving technology. Take those out and the market is paying a modest multiple for the cloud business itself.

A share in Nebius is a share in everything it owns. So to see what the market is paying for the cloud business alone, take the price of the whole company and set the other two aside. The answer depends mostly on ClickHouse, which could be worth anything from $1.5bn to about $14bn.

06ARR

Annualised run-rate revenue (ARR) has been the headline number for Nebius, and for our October preview. From 2027 Nebius will guide on revenue, pre-tax earnings and capacity instead.

Two ways to read it

ARR takes one month's AI cloud revenue and multiplies it by 12. It reached $6.4bn in September, up from $3.0bn in June. Nebius says it no longer reflects the business: it rests on a single month, and partnership revenue can make it jumpy. Nebius has not said how much of September's $6.4bn the partnership deal accounts for.

The generous reading

Revenue and profit are what mature companies report. They are audited, harder to flatter and easier to compare with peers. As Nebius moves towards profit, this is the natural switch.

The cautious reading

The metric was dropped in the same quarter its target was missed. A number investors had focused on is no longer there to hold management to.

07Nvidia

Nvidia's results back the neocloud case

Nvidia reported on 17 November, and it was a strong quarter. Revenue was $115.8bn, up 103% on a year ago, and Nvidia now expects about 75% growth next year. The part that matters most for Nebius: revenue from AI clouds, industry and enterprise, the group the neoclouds sit in, nearly tripled to $47.5bn, and Nvidia said the growth came mainly from Vera Rubin shipments to leading neoclouds. It said its chips are fully used at every neocloud it serves, and that Vera Rubin racks, its newest system, are already running at Nebius.

It also said rental prices are rising. Rates for its B300 chips are up more than 60% since launch, and older Hopper chips have been re-let at up to twice their original price. That supports the centre of this review: rent per megawatt holding up as Nebius signs new deals. Nebius, CoreWeave and IREN shares rose 8% to 10% in after-hours trading.

The results followed a shock. On 12 November, the same day Nebius reported, chief executive Jensen Huang resigned with immediate effect, following revelations about his personal conduct, and finance chief Colette Kress became acting chief executive. Nvidia has helped build the "neocloud" sector by investing in these companies and backing some of their projects, so the news hit Nebius harder than most. These were Nvidia's first results under Kress.

Selective, and why that can help

Nvidia said it will be “disciplined and selective” in backing neoclouds, and its written commentary warned that many are growing faster than their balance sheets can support. Its commitments to them fell to $32bn, from $36bn three months earlier, and only apply once a partner has its financing in place and its capacity running. We read this as a smaller budget aimed at fewer, stronger companies. On the call, Kress said Nvidia will still help very strong customers that need financing support. With contracts from Microsoft and Meta and growing customer prepayments, Nebius is one of the strongest, so it could take a larger share, although Nvidia has not said how the budget is split. Nvidia also said it is reviewing its support as other sources of capital open up, and Nebius is moving towards funding itself: it expects prepayments and loans secured on its contracts to cover 75% to 85% of 2027 capex.

What it does not change

Nebius's signed contracts, its prepayments and its agreements with Microsoft and Meta are already in place, and it says its chip supply for 2027 is confirmed. The Nebius figures in this review are unchanged.

08Funding

Most of 2027 is covered. The rest needs raising.

Nebius expects $17bn to $20bn of customer prepayments in 2027, up from about $10bn this year. Together with loans secured against long contracts with large customers such as Microsoft and Meta, it expects these to cover 75% to 85% of 2027 capex.

That leaves 15% to 25% to find from its cash, other borrowing or new shares. One independent estimate puts it at $6bn to $11bn. Nebius says it will raise when the price is right, and that the size of the gap partly depends on which contracts it chooses to sign.

Our conviction in the business has not changed: the economics got stronger this quarter. What changed is the market's mood, towards Nebius and towards the whole neocloud sector after Nvidia's leadership crisis. That puts Nebius in Patience: a business we believe in, going through a period the market does not like.

This is where the core-and-satellite approach helps. The core position is held through periods like this. Any trading happens only in the smaller satellite slice around it.

The thesisTEI matrix: Patience

The case rests on Nebius renting AI computing power at rising prices, with customers paying for much of the build in advance, and earning more from each megawatt through software like Token Factory. This quarter strengthened that. The question now is delivery and funding: can Nebius switch its sites on as planned, and fund the years until revenue catches up with spending without too many new shares?

The case for

  • Prices, payback and prepayments all improved. Demand is not the problem.
  • Customers now fund much of the build, reducing the need for outside money.
  • Revenue guided to roughly quadruple in 2027, with a date for profit, and a guide that looks cautious.
  • Token Factory and partnerships could earn more per megawatt than renting chips.
  • Owned sites give more control over delivery.

The case against

  • Underlying growth slowed to about 13% without the partnership deal.
  • Capex stays at $40bn+ a year while it grows, so cash stays negative for years.
  • Contracted backlog grew only 3% in Q3, to $38.7bn, although large deals followed in early Q4.
  • Debt now exceeds cash, shares are up 16% in a year and more raising is likely.
  • $4.0bn owed by customers had not been collected at the quarter end.
  • Servers are written off over five years, which flatters profit if they age faster.
  • Heavy reliance on Nvidia, now going through a leadership crisis.

Glossary
ARR
Annualised run-rate revenue. One month's AI cloud revenue multiplied by 12.
Asset-light model
Partners pay for the capacity and run it on Nebius's design and software. Nebius earns fees without owning the building.
Capex
Capital expenditure. Money spent on long-lasting assets such as data centres and chips.
Contract value per MW
The yearly value of a contract for each megawatt of capacity. Nebius's measure of rent per room.
Convertible bonds
Loans that can turn into shares later, usually if the share price rises.
Deferred revenue
Money billed or received in advance for services not yet delivered. It becomes revenue as the service is provided.
Depreciation
Spreading the cost of an asset over its useful life in the profit figures, rather than counting it all at once.
Adjusted EBITDA
Profit before interest, tax, depreciation and some other items. A rough measure of operating profit.
Gigawatt (GW)
1,000 megawatts. Nebius plans to add more than 1 GW of capacity a year.
Neocloud
A specialist AI cloud company that rents out GPU computing power, such as Nebius and CoreWeave.
Payback
How long a deal takes to earn back the cost of building and running its capacity.
Prepayment
Money a customer pays up front, before the capacity is delivered.
RPO (backlog)
Remaining performance obligations. Signed contract revenue not yet earned.
Token Factory
Nebius's service for running AI models for customers, charged per use.
