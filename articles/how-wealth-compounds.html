How wealth actually compounds

Compounding works like a snowball rolling down a hill: the bigger it gets, the faster it grows. That is why the same rate of return turns into a curve instead of a straight line, and why money invested five years earlier ends up worth far more than five extra years of saving would suggest.

Compounding works like a snowball rolling down a hill. The bigger it gets, the more snow it picks up with each turn, so it grows faster and faster without doing anything differently. Money does the same thing: growth on top of growth, again and again. The pot is the snowball: everything invested so far, plus everything it has already earned.

Every year, the pot is a little bigger, so the same rate of growth adds a bigger number than the year before. Nothing else is happening. There is no trick, and no special skill involved.

Take £200 a month at 7% a year. Over 40 years that puts in £96,000, and the pot ends at about £494,000: around four fifths of it is money the pot earned on its own. The sliders change the amount, the rate and the time, and the shape stays the same: a slow start, then a curve that keeps steepening.

What delaying by one year, or five, does to a pot by retirement.

Because each year's growth joins the pot, money invested early gets more years of growth on growth than money invested later, like a snowball let loose sooner down the same hill. The gap this creates has little to do with how much was put in. It is about how many extra years that money had to keep growing.

Waiting five years to start is not five years of missed saving. It is five years of missed growth on growth. In this example, starting at 30 instead of 25 puts in £12,000 less by 65, and leaves the pot about £152,000 smaller.

The first rule of compounding: never interrupt it unnecessarily.Charlie Munger
