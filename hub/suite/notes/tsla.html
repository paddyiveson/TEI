Start Here: What Is Tesla Being Priced As?

Tesla (NASDAQ: TSLA) is, today, still overwhelmingly a car company: of the $28.24 billion it billed in Q2 2026, roughly 77% came from selling and leasing vehicles. But the stock does not trade like a car company. Ford trades around 7-8x forward earnings and General Motors around 11-12x. Tesla trades around 300x trailing earnings — a multiple that only makes sense if you believe the market is pricing something else entirely: a future robotaxi network, a humanoid-robot workforce (Optimus), and an AI/compute stack (Dojo, FSD) that could scale far beyond car sales.

Founded by Elon Musk (CEO) and a team that has since scattered across his other ventures, Tesla remains the largest EV maker in the US and a top-three global EV maker by volume, alongside a fast-growing energy storage business (Megapack, Powerwall) that is quietly one of the best-margin parts of the company. The question this note tries to answer isn't "is Tesla a good car company" — it's whether the AI/robotics optionality embedded in the share price is real enough, and close enough, to justify the multiple being paid for it today.

In Plain English

Why did the stock fall on a record-revenue quarter?

Q2 2026 revenue of $28.24 billion was an all-time high, up 26% year-over-year. But operating income was only $398 million — an operating margin of just 1.4%, down from 4.1% a year earlier — because capital spending on autonomy and robotics jumped 142% to $5.79 billion for the quarter, and the high-margin regulatory-credit revenue that used to cushion earnings shrank to $146 million. The market's read: Tesla is spending car-company cash to fund an AI-company bet, and investors are being asked to trust the payoff without seeing it yet.

In Plain English

What are "regulatory credits" and why do they matter here?

Automakers that don't sell enough EVs to meet emissions rules can buy compliance credits from those that do — Tesla, selling nothing but EVs, has earned billions of dollars of almost pure-profit revenue this way over the years (over $2.7 billion annually at the recent peak). The 2025 federal rollback of fuel-economy penalties has sharply reduced other automakers' need to buy those credits, so this cushion is fading at the same time core auto margins are compressing — a double hit to reported profitability that has nothing to do with how many cars Tesla actually sells.

The Core Debate: Two Very Different Companies Under One Ticker

Every Tesla bull and bear case starts from the same financials and reaches opposite conclusions, because they're implicitly modelling two different businesses. Toggle between them below.

Financial Position: Growing Revenue, Shrinking Margin

Q2 2026 was genuinely two stories at once. Deliveries of 480,126 vehicles beat estimates and grew 25% year-over-year — Tesla's strongest Q2 ever and its first positive year-over-year delivery quarter after two consecutive annual declines (2024 and 2025). Inventory fell as Tesla delivered more cars than it built. But automotive gross margin excluding regulatory credits came in at 16.3%, down 290 basis points from Q1 2026 and less than half its 2022 peak of roughly 28-29%.

Two consecutive annual delivery declines (2024, then 2025) were followed by a genuine 2026 recovery: Q1 2026 deliveries rose ~6% YoY and Q2 2026 rose 25% YoY. Part of that rebound looks durable (cheaper Model 3/Y "Standard" trims starting at $39,990, fading boycott effects in Europe), and part of it looks temporary (elevated gasoline prices tied to the Iran conflict pulling marginal buyers toward EVs, and inventory drawdown that can't repeat every quarter). Robotaxi & FSD: Real Progress, Still Mostly Supervised

Tesla's Robotaxi service launched in Austin on June 22, 2025, with a paid safety monitor in the front passenger seat and immediate NHTSA scrutiny after early riders reported wrong-lane driving and unsafe drop-offs. Since then the service has expanded to seven US metro areas — Austin, Dallas, Houston, Miami, Orlando, Tampa, and the San Francisco Bay Area — up from three at the start of 2026, and Tesla has begun integrating unsupervised (no in-car monitor) vehicles into the fleet, starting in Austin in January 2026.

The honest reading of the numbers: unsupervised miles are a small fraction of total Robotaxi miles logged so far, and independent reporting has repeatedly found the "vast majority" of Austin rides still carry a human safety monitor, even as Tesla touts unsupervised-mile growth of "over 10% a week." Tesla has also disclosed, after NHTSA pressure, that at least two Robotaxi incidents involved remote teleoperators taking over (including one where a remote operator caused a crash in Houston) — a reminder that "unsupervised" doesn't yet mean "unmonitored." Management's own guidance is that broader unsupervised FSD in customer-owned vehicles begins a "gradual rollout" in Q4 2026, in geographies already validated by the Robotaxi fleet.

What's genuinely unclear: Tesla has not published a ride-level breakdown of supervised vs. truly unsupervised trips, an official fleet size, or a timeline with named cities for full safety-driver removal nationally. The "10%-a-week" unsupervised-mile growth figure is a company-reported statistic without independent verification, and 380,000 unsupervised miles is a rounding error next to the billions of real-world miles regulators typically want to see before trusting a system at scale (for comparison, Waymo has logged tens of millions of fully driverless miles). Treat robotaxi as a real but early-stage program, not a proven revenue line.

Optimus: The Most Speculative Line in the Story

Musk has stated that Optimus V3 production begins at the Fremont factory in summer 2026, reaching high volume by summer 2027, with a stated long-run capacity ambition of 1 million units/year at Fremont's Line One and up to 10 million units/year once Gigafactory Texas capacity is added. Musk has separately suggested Optimus could eventually be Tesla's largest product line by revenue, dwarfing the car business.

What's genuinely unclear — and should be treated as such: None of Tesla's prior "next year" production promises (the original Cybertruck timeline, the 2019 "1 million robotaxis by 2020" claim, full self-driving "next year" for several years running) have landed on schedule. There is no independently verified Optimus unit-cost, no confirmed customer other than internal Tesla factory use, and no revenue reported from Optimus today. A 10-million-unit annual capacity target is a capacity ambition, not a demand forecast, and the gap between the two has been Tesla's most consistent execution risk. This note treats any specific Optimus revenue or unit projection — bull or bear — as a guess dressed up as a number, not a forecast to build a valuation on.

Energy Storage: The Quiet, High-Margin Growth Story

While Robotaxi and Optimus dominate the headlines, Tesla Energy is the part of the AI/robotics narrative that already shows up in the numbers. Full-year 2025 storage deployments hit a record 46.7 GWh, energy revenue reached $12.8 billion (+27% YoY), and the segment posted a record 29.8% gross margin — nearly double the auto business's margin. Energy now represents about 13% of total company revenue, up from 10% in 2024. Q2 2026 alone saw 13.5 GWh deployed, up 41% year-over-year, with energy revenue of $3.14 billion.

The Musk Factor: Governance, Attention, and Brand Risk

Tesla's investment case cannot be separated from its CEO's role across at least five other ventures (SpaceX, xAI, Neuralink, The Boring Company, and various political activities), and 2025-26 has been a live demonstration of both sides of that dependency.

Governance

The $1 trillion pay package

In November 2025, Tesla shareholders approved a new Musk compensation package with vesting tied to market-cap milestones reportedly reaching as high as $8.5 trillion, passing with roughly 75% support. Both major proxy advisory firms, ISS and Glass Lewis, recommended shareholders vote against it, and Musk publicly called the two firms "corporate terrorists" in the run-up to the vote — a governance fight unusually public even by Tesla's standards. Shareholders also approved letting Tesla invest directly in Musk's separate company, xAI, raising further related-party questions about where Tesla's capital and Musk's attention are really going.

Brand & Demand

Europe shows the brand-damage effect can cut both ways

Tesla's European sales fell as much as 49% year-over-year in early 2025 amid protests and consumer backlash tied to Musk's political activity in several markets. By March 2026, several European markets had swung back to sharp year-over-year growth (France +203%, Norway +178%) — but largely off that depressed 2025 base, with newer/cheaper trims and elevated gas prices also contributing. The Netherlands and Switzerland were still declining as of the most recent data. Read this as evidence the brand-damage effect is real and geographically uneven, not as evidence it's fully behind the company.

The pattern to watch is less any single controversy and more the compounding effect: a CEO managing six ventures and an increasingly public political profile, a board that has repeatedly been challenged on independence, and a customer base in at least some markets that has shown it will vote with its wallet. None of this shows up as a line item in the income statement, but it has shown up directly in delivery numbers before, and could again.

Near-Term Catalysts

Q2 2026 delivery inflection: First positive YoY delivery quarter (+25%) after two consecutive annual declines — confirms whether the cheaper trims and demand recovery are real.

Robotaxi expansion to 7 metros: Austin, Dallas, Houston, Miami, Orlando, Tampa, and the SF Bay Area now live, up from 3 cities at the start of 2026.

$1 trillion Musk pay package approved (Nov 2025): Removes a governance overhang but ties Musk's incentives to extremely long-dated, high-bar milestones.

Optimus V3 production start (guided: summer 2026): The first real test of whether humanoid-robot production timelines hold, at Fremont.

Unsupervised personal FSD "gradual rollout" (guided: Q4 2026): Would extend unsupervised driving beyond the Robotaxi fleet into customer-owned cars for the first time, starting in validated geographies.

Megapack 3 / Megablock launch and continued energy margin trend: Tests whether the 29.8% energy gross margin holds as competition and price cuts intensify.

Q3 2026 earnings (date not yet announced): First look at whether the Q2 margin collapse was a one-off capex spike or a new baseline.

Any material Robotaxi safety incident or NHTSA enforcement action: Downside catalyst risk given 14+ disclosed Austin crashes and an active federal investigation history.

Key Risks

300x trailing P/E 1.4% operating margin Negative free cash flow Optimus/robotaxi execution risk Musk time-allocation & governance risk Regulatory-credit revenue fading

The valuation has almost no margin for disappointment. At ~300x trailing earnings, Tesla is priced on a story, not on current cash flow. If Optimus or unsupervised FSD timelines slip again — as they repeatedly have — there is very little earnings support underneath the current share price, and the stock has already shown it can move double digits in a single session on a margin miss alone.

The core auto business is still under margin pressure. 16.3% auto gross margin (ex-credits) is roughly half the 2022 peak, and the fading regulatory-credit cushion removes a source of near-pure-profit revenue that has historically flattered reported earnings. A demand recovery in units does not automatically mean a profit recovery.

Robotaxi and Optimus are both still substantially unproven at scale. Unsupervised Robotaxi miles are a small fraction of total fleet miles, teleoperator interventions have already caused at least one disclosed crash, and Optimus has no independently verified cost, customer, or revenue today. Both are real programs with real progress — neither is a proven business yet.

Governance and key-person risk sit unusually close to the surface. A CEO running six-plus ventures, a board repeatedly challenged by proxy advisors on independence, and a pay package tied to multi-trillion-dollar market-cap milestones all concentrate risk in ways that are hard to model financially but have shown up directly in delivery numbers before (the 2025 European boycott).

This is a stock where the bull and bear cases are not really disagreeing about the facts — both sides can cite the same Q2 2026 print. They disagree about which business Tesla actually is. Position sizing should assume the stock can re-rate sharply in either direction around any single data point (a delivery report, a margin print, a Robotaxi safety headline, an Optimus production update) because so much of the current price is narrative rather than earnings.

What would move this from watchlist to portfolio consideration: (1) auto gross margin (ex-credits) stabilizing or recovering for two consecutive quarters rather than continuing to compress; (2) an independently verifiable unsupervised-mile figure from Robotaxi that shows the safety-monitor ratio meaningfully declining, not just company-stated weekly growth rates; (3) Optimus V3 actually entering production on the stated summer 2026 timeline with a disclosed unit cost; or (4) the stock re-rating to a multiple where the AI/robotics optionality is priced as a call option rather than as a near-certainty. Without at least one of these, the current risk/reward does not justify adding it to the fund over existing holdings.

Bottom Line: The Businesses Are Real. The Price Assumes They All Work.

Tesla in August 2026 is a genuinely two-sided story. The car business is recovering — deliveries grew for the first time in two years, cheaper trims are selling, and the brand-damage effect in Europe looks like it's fading in some markets — but it's recovering into structurally lower margins than it had in 2022, with a fading regulatory-credit cushion on top. Energy storage is the one part of the "AI-adjacent" story that's already delivering: real growth, real 29.8% margins, and a genuine second profit engine. Robotaxi and Optimus are the parts still mostly ahead of the company, not behind it — real progress, real expansion, but neither yet a proven, independently-verified, revenue-generating business at any meaningful scale.

Near term: Q3 2026 earnings and any concrete Optimus production milestone are the two most likely catalysts to move the stock sharply in either direction over the next one to two quarters.

Long term: if Robotaxi's unsupervised fleet scales safely, Optimus hits even a fraction of its stated volume targets, and auto margins stabilize, the current multiple could look justified in hindsight. If any one of those three stalls the way full self-driving timelines have stalled before, the multiple has a long way to compress. Honestly: the AI/robotics optionality specifically is not a number TEI can pin down with any real precision right now — and any note that pretends otherwise, in either direction, is overselling its own confidence.

TEI positioning: Watchlist - not a current Growth Fund holding. High priority to keep monitoring, given how much this name comes up in client questions, but not close to a fund add today. If added, this would sit at Tail tier within the Growth Fund sleeve — the optionality case is genuine, but a ~300x multiple on a core business still working through margin compression is too much unresolved risk for a Core or Anchor sizing, and TEI capital is currently better deployed in the fund's existing higher-conviction holdings. This is a capital-allocation call, not a rejection of the thesis. Monitor: Q3 2026 earnings, any independently verified unsupervised-Robotaxi mileage disclosure, and Optimus V3's actual production start — whichever comes first should prompt a formal watchlist review.
