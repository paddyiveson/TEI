Start Here: Why Does the Ocean Floor Need Its Own Defence Industry?

Kraken Robotics makes two things that almost nobody outside the defence industry has ever heard of, and both are becoming critically important: batteries that work at the crushing pressure of the deep ocean, and sonar systems precise enough to tell a naval mine apart from a rock on the seabed, from a moving vehicle, in real time. It does not build submarines or underwater drones itself. It builds the power source and the "eyes" that go inside them - and it has become the supplier of choice for some of the most important underwater vehicle programmes in the Western world.

The Problem

The Ocean Floor Is Suddenly a Military Frontier

For decades, the underwater domain was the quiet corner of military strategy - submarines moved slowly and secretly, and nobody worried much about what was happening on the seabed itself. That has changed dramatically and recently. Undersea internet cables carrying the majority of global data traffic have been mysteriously cut or damaged in the Baltic Sea. Chinese research submarines have been mapping the Arctic seafloor. And naval mines - cheap, difficult to detect, capable of closing an entire shipping strait - have re-emerged as one of the most cost-effective weapons available to a smaller military trying to deter a much larger one.

Detecting what is on the seabed is a genuinely hard problem. Water absorbs and scatters light, so cameras are useless beyond a few metres. Radio signals barely travel underwater at all. The only practical way to "see" underwater at range is sound - sonar - and building sonar precise enough to reliably tell a mine from a rock, an old shipwreck, or a piece of marine debris, at the resolution needed to act on that information in real time, is extraordinarily difficult engineering.

Think of it this way: Imagine trying to identify a coin lying on a football pitch, from a hundred metres away, in complete darkness, using only echoes bounced off it. Now imagine you need to do that while moving, from underwater, distinguishing a genuinely dangerous object from thousands of harmless ones nearby, in a strait carrying a fifth of the world's oil shipments, with a "close enough" answer not being good enough. That is the resolution problem synthetic aperture sonar exists to solve.

The Solution

What Kraken Actually Builds

Kraken's core technology is Synthetic Aperture Sonar (SAS) - a sonar technique that combines multiple sonar "pings" taken as a vehicle moves through the water into a single, ultra-high-resolution image of the seabed, similar in principle to how satellite radar builds detailed ground images by combining many passes. Kraken's SAS systems produce imagery at a constant resolution of roughly 3cm by 3cm, precise enough to resolve mine-like objects and even subsea cables as small as 5cm in diameter - a level of detail conventional sonar cannot match.

The second, less glamorous but arguably more important half of the business is pressure-tolerant subsea batteries, sold under the SeaPower brand. Every unmanned underwater vehicle (UUV) needs a power source, and building batteries that can operate reliably at ocean depths - where pressure would crush a standard battery casing - is a genuinely hard materials and engineering problem. Kraken's polymer-based sealing approach has effectively given it a multi-year head start that competitors, most of whom are focused on electric vehicle batteries rather than subsea-specific engineering, have struggled to replicate.

Neither product is glamorous on its own. Together, they are the two components that determine whether an underwater drone can see clearly and stay submerged long enough to matter - which is exactly why Kraken has found itself embedded inside some of the most important underwater vehicle programmes being built today.

The Central Relationship

Inside Anduril's Underwater Fleet

Anduril Industries is one of the largest privately held defence technology companies in America, valued at tens of billions of dollars and building autonomous systems across air, land, sea, and now the undersea domain. Anduril does not manufacture its own subsea batteries or sonar from scratch - for these mission-critical subsystems, it has turned to Kraken. This is the single most important commercial relationship in Kraken's story, and it now spans two major underwater vehicle programmes plus a newly announced small-vessel partnership.

The Strategic Read

Why This Matters Structurally

Kraken's battery technology remains one of the few mission-critical subsystems Anduril still sources externally rather than building in-house. As unit volumes scale from prototype to serial production on both programmes, Kraken's shipments scale proportionally - and once a battery system is certified and operationally validated on a platform, replacing it becomes non-trivial.

Volume-linked revenue, high switching cost once certified

What this is not: Kraken does not have a formal exclusivity agreement or long-term take-or-pay contract locking in this relationship - it is a qualified, embedded supplier relationship built on repeated orders and platform integration, not a guaranteed revenue stream. If Anduril's own production schedules slip, or if Anduril chooses to vertically integrate battery or sonar production in-house at scale, Kraken's growth assumptions would need to be revisited. This is addressed further in the Risks section.

The Strait of Hormuz Crisis, and What Kraken Has Actually Proven

In early 2026, the Strait of Hormuz - the narrow waterway through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes - was closed after Iran deployed naval mines in response to coordinated US and Israeli strikes. For several tense weeks, the world watched the US Navy and allies scramble to clear those mines using a combination of AI-assisted sonar, uncrewed surface vessels, and autonomous underwater vehicles - a live, high-stakes demonstration of exactly why undersea mine countermeasure capability has become one of the most urgently funded categories in Western defence spending. The systems publicly reported as deployed in that specific operation were built by other US primes (Textron's uncrewed surface vessels, RTX's towed sonar, General Dynamics' AUVs) - Kraken's role in the Hormuz operation itself has not been confirmed. What the crisis did do, unambiguously, is validate the strategic case for the entire category Kraken operates in: the world does not currently have enough undersea sensors, uncrewed vehicles, or mine countermeasure capacity, and every Western navy now knows it.

The Actual Proof Point - REPMUS 2025, Portugal

Kraken's Sonar Has Already Been Tested Against Real Mine-Like Objects, Repeatedly, in NATO's Largest Unmanned Systems Exercise

REPMUS is NATO's largest annual robotic and autonomous systems exercise, held in Portugal with naval teams from over 20 countries. Kraken has now supported this exercise for four consecutive years. In the October 2025 running, 10 separate teams used Kraken SAS systems - double the number from the prior year - supported by seven international naval teams and three different UUV manufacturers, with Kraken sonar integrated onto four distinct UUV vehicle classes. This is the real, independently observable evidence that Kraken's sonar performs its stated mission: finding and classifying mine-like objects on the seabed, reliably, under NATO evaluation.

The distinction matters for an honest investment case: Kraken's growth thesis does not depend on having been the specific supplier inside the Hormuz operation. Higher-margin, diversifying beyond pure defence into offshore energy

Roughly 70% of Kraken's revenue has historically come from military and defence-adjacent customers, with the balance from commercial applications including offshore wind farm surveying (boulder detection, cable route planning) and subsea infrastructure inspection. The Covelya acquisition (detailed below) is explicitly designed to broaden this commercial base and reduce dependence on any single defence programme's timing.

Profitability: Adjusted EBITDA Positive, GAAP Still Marginal

Kraken has been Adjusted EBITDA positive and improving for several years, with Q3 2025 Adjusted EBITDA of C$7.5M (+64% YoY) on a 59% gross margin - genuinely strong operating leverage at that point in the cycle. Q1 2026 showed a step back in both gross margin (56%, down from 63%) and Adjusted EBITDA margin (14%, down from 17%), which management and covering analysts attribute to scale-up costs ahead of the Covelya integration and mix shift toward lower-margin product deliveries. Management's own FY26 target is 24-29% Adjusted EBITDA margin for the combined company - meaningfully above the Q1 print, meaning the company needs to demonstrate margin recovery through the remainder of 2026 for the current growth-plus-margin narrative to hold. On a GAAP net income basis, reported earnings remain marginal and volatile quarter to quarter - this is a name to track on Adjusted EBITDA trajectory and cash generation, not GAAP EPS, at this stage.

Key Catalysts to Watch

Covelya Group acquisition closed (July 2, 2026): Regulatory and exchange approvals received; the largest transaction in Kraken's history now fully integrated into the reporting entity. Updated combined-company guidance of C$290-310M now the operative target to track against.

Anduril small USV partnership announced (April 2026): Extends the relationship beyond Ghost Shark and Dive-LD into uncrewed surface vessels, broadening Kraken's addressable content per Anduril programme.

REPMUS 2025 record deployment (October 2025): Fourth consecutive year, double the prior year's team participation - the clearest independent, NATO-observed proof point of Kraken's sonar performing its core mission.

NASDAQ uplisting: Kraken has filed SEC registration statements and stated an intention to uplist from OTC/TSX-V to a major US exchange. As of this note, the uplisting had not yet been confirmed complete - successful completion would materially improve liquidity and open the stock to a much larger pool of US institutional capital.

Margin recovery through FY2026: Q1 2026 showed gross and Adjusted EBITDA margin compression against management's own 24-29% full-year target. Subsequent quarters need to show the trajectory reversing for the growth-plus-margin thesis to hold together.

Ghost Shark and Dive-LD serial production ramp: Both Anduril facilities (Sydney, Australia and Rhode Island, US) are scaling through 2026. Confirmed unit production numbers, as they become available, are the clearest read on whether Kraken's volume-linked battery revenue is tracking the bull case.

Canada's submarine and defence recapitalisation: Canada has accelerated its NATO defence spending timetable toward 2% of GDP, with a reported ~$100 billion submarine programme under discussion - a potential home-market tailwind for a Newfoundland-headquartered company with an already-established domestic defence relationship.

Risks: Why This Sits in the Tail

Anduril customer concentration Small-cap illiquidity Margin compression Q1'26 Program timing risk Covelya integration risk FX exposure (CAD/USD)

Anduril concentration is real and structural. A meaningful and growing share of Kraken's revenue and order pipeline is tied to a single customer's production schedule. Anduril has also demonstrated, across its broader business, a willingness to vertically integrate subsystems it considers strategically important once volumes justify it. Kraken's battery technology is one of the few areas Anduril still sources externally - but that is a present-tense fact, not a permanent guarantee. Any move by Anduril to bring battery or sonar production in-house, or any slippage in the Ghost Shark or Dive-LD production timelines, would directly affect Kraken's growth trajectory.

The stock is genuinely small, illiquid, and volatile. At a ~$1.46B market cap trading primarily on the OTCQB and TSX Venture Exchange, KRKNF has far lower trading liquidity than the fund's larger-cap holdings, and the 52-week range of $2.36 to $8.13 reflects that volatility directly - the stock is currently trading roughly 42% below its 52-week high, which is the specific reason it sits in the tail of the portfolio rather than a larger position today.

Margins compressed in the most recent quarter. Gross margin fell from 63% to 56% and Adjusted EBITDA margin from 17% to 14% year-on-year in Q1 2026, against a full-year management target of 24-29%. This needs to reverse through the rest of 2026 for the growth-plus-profitability narrative to hold - it is the single most important number to watch in upcoming quarterly results.

Covelya integration carries execution risk. At C$615M, the acquisition is larger than Kraken's entire pre-deal market capitalisation was for much of its recent history. Integrating a company of this scale - systems, people, customer relationships, reporting - inside a single fiscal year is a genuine operational challenge, and the C$290-310M pro-forma guidance assumes that integration proceeds smoothly.

Currency exposure. Kraken reports in Canadian dollars but sells substantially into US and international defence markets priced in other currencies. CAD/USD movements affect both reported revenue growth rates and margin outcomes in ways that can obscure the underlying operational trend quarter to quarter.

This is precisely why Kraken sits in the tail of the TEI Growth Fund rather than a larger core position: the long-term structural thesis - embedded supplier to the highest-priority underwater defence programmes in the Western alliance, proven technology, a large and growing backlog - is genuinely compelling. But the stock is currently out of market favour, down sharply from its 52-week high, carrying real customer concentration and margin execution risk, and trading with the liquidity profile of a genuine small-cap. Size accordingly and expect volatility.

Bottom Line: A Proven Niche Supplier, Currently Priced Like Nobody's Watching

Kraken Robotics has spent the better part of a decade solving two genuinely hard engineering problems - subsea power and underwater imaging - and has ended up embedded inside the underwater vehicle programmes of Anduril, one of the most important private defence companies in America, at exactly the moment Western navies are waking up to how underprepared they are for undersea warfare and mine countermeasures. The REPMUS track record is real, independently observable proof that the sonar works. The Anduril relationship, while not contractually guaranteed, has scaled from a single battery order to two flagship AUV programmes and a new USV partnership in under two years.

The honest read on Hormuz: The crisis was a genuine strategic catalyst for the entire undersea defence category - but Kraken's specific hardware involvement in that operation has not been confirmed, and the investment case should rest on the demonstrated NATO exercise track record and the Anduril platform relationship, not on an unverified claim about a single geopolitical event.

The near-term test is margin recovery. Q1 2026's gross and EBITDA margin compression, against a full-year target that requires meaningful improvement from here, is the clearest thing to watch over the next two quarters. If Covelya integrates cleanly and margins recover toward the 24-29% target while the Anduril volume ramp continues, this is a name that could re-rate significantly from current, out-of-favour levels. If margins stay compressed or the Anduril ramp slips, the stock's small-cap volatility will likely punish that disappointment sharply.

TEI positioning: Growth Fund holding, currently sized in the tail given low market favour, high volatility, and the specific near-term execution questions around Covelya integration and margin recovery. The long-term thesis - proven undersea sensing technology, embedded inside the highest-priority defence supply chains in the Western alliance - remains intact.
