Pension vs LISA: which should you use?

A pension is easier to get money into. A Lifetime ISA is cheaper to get money out of. For most employees, the employer's contribution is what decides which one comes out ahead.

Every UK savings wrapper runs the same basic trade. It adds something on top of what is paid in, and in exchange it restricts when that money can be taken back out. A workplace pension and a Lifetime ISA, usually shortened to a LISA, both run on this trade. They just run it on very different terms.

The table only goes so far. To see how the rules play out, follow one person through them: an employee earning £40,000 a year, so a basic-rate taxpayer, aged 30. Whatever they put away stays invested until 60 and grows at the same 5% a year in either wrapper, so growth never decides the comparison on its own.

8×The pension can take eight times as much in a year as the LISA can.
The pension annual allowance is £60,000 or 100% of earnings, whichever is lower. The pension figure includes basic-rate tax relief. The LISA limit of £4,000 sits inside the £20,000 overall ISA allowance.

That ceiling matters for anyone saving more than a few thousand pounds a year. But most people are not near either limit, so the more useful question is what the same amount of take-home pay buys in each.

Salary sacrifice: £4,000 ÷ 0.72 ≈ £5,556, because the sacrificed pay avoids 20% income tax and 8% employee National Insurance. Relief at source: £4,000 ÷ 0.8 = £5,000. Employer contribution shown as 3% of a £40,000 salary, £1,200.

There are two common ways pension contributions leave a payslip. With relief at source, the saver pays in from take-home pay and the provider claims 20% basic-rate relief from HMRC, so £4,000 becomes £5,000: exactly what the LISA bonus does. With salary sacrifice, the saver gives up pay before tax and National Insurance are worked out, so the same hit to take-home pay buys more pension. Many employers then add their own contribution on top, and nothing like that ever reaches a LISA.

15%Roughly what income tax takes from the whole pension pot: nothing on the first quarter, 20% on the other three quarters.
Assumes the State Pension uses up the personal allowance, so every pound of taxable pension income is taxed at the 20% basic rate. Higher income in retirement would raise the rate; lower income could reduce it.

This is where the LISA earns its place. Its bonus goes in once and is never taxed again, while a pension's tax relief is partly paid back on the way out. Without any employer money, salary sacrifice ends with about £4,720 against the LISA's £5,000, and relief at source with about £4,250. Add an employer contribution and the picture changes.

Same 5% yearly growth on both, so growth scales the gap but never changes which side wins. Employer contribution as a % of a £40,000 salary. Pension tax on the way out as in Scene 3. With no employer money the LISA stays ahead; the pension pulls level at about 2.2% (relief at source) or 0.8% (salary sacrifice). From your Wealth OS profile, income and pension contribution rule. The same £4,000 of take-home pay into each, 5% a year growth on both, about 15% tax on the pension when it comes out. An illustration, not a forecast or a recommendation.

Pension money unlocks at 55 today and at 57 from 6 April 2028. A LISA unlocks at 60, or earlier if the money goes towards a first home worth up to £450,000. Taken out any other way, the LISA money is still there, but reaching it early comes at a cost.

Two rule changes are already on the way. The government plans to replace the LISA with a simpler First-Time Buyer ISA, expected from April 2028. Anyone who already holds a LISA can keep paying into it under today's rules.

The second affects salary sacrifice. From 6 April 2029, the National Insurance saving will apply only to the first £2,000 sacrificed each year. Above that, each extra pound sacrificed still costs 80p of take-home pay rather than 72p. In this example, £4,000 of take-home pay would then put about £5,200 into the pension instead of £5,556. The pension's head start shrinks most for people sacrificing larger amounts.

What this does not say. None of this says whether a pension or a LISA fits a particular person better. The example is one basic-rate employee with one set of assumptions; higher-rate relief, a different employer scheme, a house purchase or a different income in retirement would all change the numbers. It shows how each wrapper's mechanics work, which is where any comparison has to start.

Summary

01

A pension is easier to get money into.

Its yearly limit is far higher than the LISA's £4,000, and through salary sacrifice the same take-home pay buys more of it.

02

A LISA is cheaper to get money out of.

It is tax-free after 60, while a basic-rate pension loses roughly 15% to income tax on the way out.

03

The employer contribution usually decides it.

Without one, the LISA ends slightly ahead for a basic-rate saver. With a typical employer contribution, the pension comes out ahead.

04

The rules are moving.

Pension access rises to 57 in 2028, the LISA is expected to give way to a First-Time Buyer ISA in 2028, and the salary sacrifice NI saving is capped from 2029.
