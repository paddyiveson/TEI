Start Here: What Is Volex?

Volex plc (LSE: VLX) designs and manufactures power cords, cable assemblies, and interconnect products — the unglamorous physical wiring that carries power and data between a device and the wall, or between one piece of equipment and another. It sells almost nothing directly to consumers; instead it makes the cords and cable assemblies that go inside or alongside products from much bigger customers, across five reporting segments: Complex Industrial Technology (which now houses its fast-growing Data Centre business), Off-Highway (defence, industrial, agricultural and construction wire harnesses), Consumer Electricals, Medical, and EV & Electrification.

Volex is run by CEO Nat Rothschild (previously the company’s executive chairman, now chief executive, with Dave Webster as chair), who has pursued a strategy of bolt-on acquisitions layered onto organic growth since taking a leading role at the company. Historically the story here was steady, unglamorous industrial consolidation. In the past two years that story has picked up a second, much more exciting chapter: hyperscale data centre operators building out AI infrastructure need more power delivered, more densely, and more reliably than a conventional data centre — and that has turned a legacy cable-assembly business into a genuine (if partial) AI-infrastructure supplier.

In Plain English

Why would an AI data centre need a cable company at all?

Training and running large AI models means packing far more powerful chips into the same rack space, which means far more electrical current has to be delivered to that rack — and far more data has to move between servers at very high speeds. Both problems are, at bottom, cable and connector problems: heavier power cords and busbars to feed the racks, and higher-speed copper interconnects (rated up to 1.6 terabits per second) to link the servers together. Volex makes both, which is why a company most people have never heard of shows up in the AI infrastructure supply chain at all.

What The Numbers Actually Show

Data Centre revenue roughly doubled — but it’s still a minority of the group

Within Complex Industrial Technology, Data Centre revenue went from about $118m in FY2025 to about $241m in FY2026 — genuinely explosive growth, and the single biggest driver of the group beating its own targets a year early. But set against total group revenue of $1,242.6m, Data Centre is still only around 19% of Volex as a whole, up from roughly 11% the year before. The other four segments — two growing, two shrinking — still make up four-fifths of the business.

The Investment Thesis: A Real AI Engine, Still Attached to a Mixed Legacy Business

Volex’s bull case rests on the idea that a decades-old, low-margin cable-assembly business has found a genuinely high-growth, high-value end market in AI data centre power and interconnect — and is now good enough at it to keep taking share as hyperscalers keep building. The bear case is that this is one fast-growing segment bolted onto a group where two other segments are shrinking, coverage is thin, and the near-term share price has already been dominated by an unrelated technical event (the AIM-to-Main Market move) rather than the AI story itself.

1. Data Centre demand is real, quantified, and (so far) not decelerating

This is not just a narrative. Data Centre revenue approximately doubled year-on-year in FY2026, driven by hyperscaler customers expanding capacity for AI infrastructure, and management said on the FY2026 call that Q1 FY2027 trading showed “demand for data centre products sustained at similar levels” — i.e., no visible slowdown in the first quarter of the new fiscal year. Volex is also moving beyond passive cables into higher-value power products, leveraging expertise from its EV business as the industry shifts from AC to DC power architectures at 400–800 volts for next-generation AI racks.

2. Management is guiding conservatively on the very segment carrying the story

This cuts both ways as evidence. Despite 56.3% organic growth in Complex Industrial Technology in FY2026, CEO Nat Rothschild described FY2027 guidance for that segment as “extremely conservative…guiding 5% growth”. That could mean prudent sandbagging after a blowout year, or it could mean management genuinely expects the exceptional FY2026 demand pulse not to repeat at the same intensity — the FY2026 results release itself flagged that “some of the exceptional demand seen this year…may not recur at the same level.” Either way, the company is not currently underwriting its own AI narrative with an aggressive forward number, which is worth taking seriously in both directions.

3. The rest of the business is a genuine mixed bag, not a clean growth story

Off-Highway grew 20.0% organically on North American expansion and EV & Electrification grew a modest 4.8%, but Medical declined 10.5% and Consumer Electricals declined 7.8% in FY2026 — both attributed to destocking, with Medical expected to stabilise in FY2027. A single-stock AI-infrastructure allocation this is not; it is an industrial conglomerate with one segment currently doing the heavy lifting.

What’s genuinely unclear: Volex does not disclose Data Centre revenue as its own reporting line — it is a sub-component of Complex Industrial Technology disclosed only as a percentage (63.0% of segment revenue in FY2026, up from 48.3%) in results commentary, not a line item in the accounts. That makes the AI-cable story directionally very credible but harder to forecast with precision than a company that breaks the segment out formally. Treat the ~$241m figure used throughout this note as a derived estimate (63.0% × $382.9m), not a reported number.

The AI Engine vs. The Rest of Volex

Volex now runs one clearly AI-linked growth engine alongside four segments with a much more mixed record. Toggle between them below.

Capital Allocation: M&A Pipeline “Remains Active”, But the Last Deal Wasn’t About AI

Volex has grown partly through bolt-on acquisitions for years, and management describes the pipeline as still active. The most recent deal, however, is a useful reminder that not every Volex acquisition feeds the AI narrative: on 14 July 2026 Volex completed the purchase of the remaining 64.3% of Kepler SignalTek (KST) — a maker of patient-connected medical cables and devices for patient monitoring, surgical procedures, cardiac therapy and ultrasound imaging, in which Volex had held a 35.7% stake since the company’s formation in 2017 — for up to $89.4m ($74.7m paid up front, plus up to $14.7m in deferred, revenue-linked consideration over two years). KST generated $51.8m of revenue in FY2026 on 10% organic growth.

Capital allocation signal: Volex halved its share buyback programme from £40m to £20m specifically to help fund the Kepler SignalTek deal, while stating it wants to keep “financial flexibility to continue investing in the group’s growth strategy.” The full-year dividend rose 6.7% to 4.8p under a stated progressive policy that weighs growth investment, capex and balance-sheet strength — a small, low-priority payout (yield under 1%) rather than an income case. Net debt fell to $121.5m (0.8x covenant leverage, down from 1.0x), giving room for further deals.

The read-through: Volex’s M&A engine is a genuine part of the medium-term growth plan (acquisitions are pencilled in for $300m of the $2bn medium-term revenue target set out below), but the most recent, largest acquisition deepened the Medical segment rather than the Data Centre one. Investors buying this stock purely for AI exposure should understand that capital is also being deployed into segments with no AI linkage at all.

Financial Position and Medium-Term Targets

FY2026 (year ended 31 March 2026) revenue was $1,242.6m, up 14.4% (14.2% organic), with underlying operating profit of $127.3m (+19.9%) and underlying operating margin of 10.2% — ahead of the company’s own 9–10% target range a year early. Underlying EPS rose 19.8% to 43.5 cents; statutory EPS rose 35.9% to 35.2 cents. Return on capital employed was 21.0%. Note the currency mix: Volex reports its income statement and balance sheet in US dollars, while its dividend is declared and its shares trade in pence sterling on the LSE — this note uses £ for market-facing figures (share price, market cap, dividend) and $ for reported financials, as the company itself does; a rough $1 = £0.75 (£1 = ~$1.34) conversion is used only where an approximate sterling figure is noted.

Capex ran at $35m (2.8% of revenue) in FY2026 and is guided at a more typical 3–4% of revenue going forward as Volex builds out manufacturing capacity for data centre and power products. Customer concentration is a real, acknowledged risk: management says its two largest accounts (in data centres and EVs) are meaningfully sized, though no other single customer exceeds 4% of group revenue — a structural feature of selling into a hyperscaler-dominated industry, not necessarily a red flag on its own, but a genuine dependency worth tracking.

The AIM → Main Market Move: The Nearest-Term Catalyst

Volex began trading on the London Stock Exchange’s Main Market on 24 July 2026, moving up from AIM after an FCA-approved prospectus, with a market cap of roughly £1.01bn at the time and shares up around 50% over the prior twelve months. The move matters mechanically: some AIM-focused, VCT- and EIS-linked funds are structurally required to sell once a stock leaves AIM, which management said drove “between 90% and 95%” of the associated selling flow by the time of the July earnings call — with a bit more still to clear. That technical overhang, not the AI story, is the leading explanation offered by management for the stock’s roughly 20–22% pullback over the past three months even after FY2026 results beat plan.

Why this is a real catalyst, not just noise: at a market cap around £1bn, Volex is a plausible candidate for FTSE 250 inclusion at FTSE Russell’s next quarterly index review — UK index reviews of this kind are typically announced in the final week of August with changes effective in the second half of September, which lines up closely with when this note is being written. Index inclusion would bring in tracker-fund buying and, likely, broader institutional coverage; it is one of the more concrete, dated events on the near-term calendar for this stock.

Analyst Coverage: Thin, Bullish, and Partly Stale

Coverage is genuinely thin for a stock now on the Main Market. The most recently updated broker targets (five ratings issued in the weeks around the FY2026 results and Main Market move) put the consensus around 594p, a “Moderate Buy” (four Buy, one Hold); a broader count from Simply Wall St puts total coverage at around 11 analysts with a consensus fair value closer to 697p, implying roughly 20% upside from current levels — the spread between these two consensus figures is itself a sign that coverage is not yet deep or fully converged post-transition.

Near-Term Catalysts

FY2026 results beat plan (25 June 2026): Revenue, margin and EPS all ahead of guidance, with Data Centre revenue roughly doubling — the print that triggered this note.

Kepler SignalTek acquisition completed (14 July 2026): Full ownership of the medical cable maker for up to $89.4m, funded partly by halving the buyback to £20m.

Move to LSE Main Market completed (24 July 2026): Up from AIM; technical selling from AIM-restricted funds is management’s stated explanation for most of the recent share-price weakness.

FTSE Russell September quarterly index review (announcement expected late August 2026, effective ~September 2026): Volex’s ~£1bn market cap makes it a plausible FTSE 250 entrant; would bring tracker-fund buying and likely broader analyst pickup.

H1 FY2027 results (16 November 2026): First full look at whether Data Centre demand is holding up against management’s own conservative ~5% Complex Industrial Technology guide, and whether the AIM-related selling flow has fully cleared.

Further M&A from an “active” pipeline: $300m of the $2bn medium-term revenue target is earmarked for acquisitions; whether the next deal is Data-Centre-linked (unlike Kepler SignalTek) will matter to the AI thesis specifically.

Key Risks

AI story is ~19% of revenue Management guides CIT at only ~5% Medical & Consumer declining Data Centre revenue is a derived estimate Thin, partly stale analyst coverage Customer concentration

The AI narrative is bigger than the AI revenue. Data Centre business is genuinely growing fast, but it is roughly a fifth of group revenue, not the majority. A re-rating priced for a pure-play AI-infrastructure name would be a mismatch for a company where four-fifths of revenue is still ordinary industrial cable-making, some of it shrinking.

Volex itself is not projecting its own hyper-growth forward. Guiding Complex Industrial Technology to roughly 5% growth in FY2027, after 56.3% organic growth in FY2026, is either prudent conservatism or a genuine signal that the FY2026 demand pulse was partly a one-off restocking/capacity event. The November 2026 interim results are the first real test of which it is.

Legacy segments are a real drag, not a rounding error. Medical (-10.5% organic) and Consumer Electricals (-7.8% organic) together were roughly a third of FY2026 group revenue. Management expects Medical to stabilise in FY2027, but that is guidance, not a result yet delivered.

Coverage is thin and not fully converged. Consensus price targets from different aggregators range from roughly 590p to 697p depending on which analyst set is used, and the most bearish-looking data point on record (Peel Hunt’s 380p) is simply stale, not a considered post-results view. Investors should not assume deep, timely sell-side scrutiny exists here the way it would for a FTSE 100 name.

Recent share-price action has been driven by index mechanics, not fundamentals — which cuts both ways. If management is right that AIM-related selling is nearly exhausted, the current pullback could be a better entry point on an unchanged thesis. If it isn’t, or if a FTSE 250 review disappoints, near-term price action could remain disconnected from the underlying business for a while longer.

This is a small/mid-cap UK industrial with one clearly exciting, well-evidenced growth segment and four segments that are ordinary-to-declining. Position sizing (if any) should reflect that Volex is currently priced and covered more like an emerging small-cap growth story than an established AI-infrastructure pure play.

What would move this from watchlist to portfolio consideration: (1) confirmed FTSE 250 inclusion at the September 2026 quarterly review; (2) H1 FY2027 results (16 November 2026) showing Complex Industrial Technology/Data Centre growth holding materially above management’s conservative ~5% guide, evidencing the demand is structural rather than a FY2026 spike; (3) Volex beginning to disclose Data Centre revenue as its own reported line rather than a derived percentage, giving genuine forecasting visibility; (4) a further pullback toward the stock’s 200-day moving average (~500–505p) that resets valuation without any change to the growth trajectory. Without at least one of these, the current risk/reward does not justify adding it to the fund over existing holdings.

Bottom Line: The AI Cable Story Is Real, But It’s Still a Minority of the Business

Volex has done something genuinely interesting: an unglamorous, decades-old cable-assembly business has built a Data Centre segment that roughly doubled in a single year on real hyperscaler AI-infrastructure demand, with management confirming that demand held up into the first quarter of the new fiscal year. That is substance, not just narrative — but it currently sits inside a group where four other segments are flat-to-declining-to-modestly-growing, where the AI-linked figure itself has to be derived rather than read off a reported line, and where management is guiding the AI-adjacent segment conservatively for the year ahead.

Near term: the two clearest tests are whether the September 2026 FTSE 250 review confirms index inclusion, and whether the 16 November 2026 half-year results show Data Centre demand outrunning management’s own cautious guide. Recent share-price weakness looks more attributable to the AIM-to-Main technical transition than to any change in the underlying growth story, but that is management’s explanation, not yet independently confirmed by a clean post-transition trading period.

Long term: if Volex keeps compounding Data Centre revenue toward a larger share of the group, hits its $2bn/12%-margin medium-term targets, and deploys its active M&A pipeline into more AI-adjacent capacity (rather than more Kepler SignalTek-style medical bolt-ons), it could plausibly grow into the AI-infrastructure allocation the current narrative already implies. Today, it is not there yet.

TEI positioning: Watchlist - not a current Growth Fund holding. Monitor closely given the two live near-term catalysts. If added, this would sit at Tail tier within the Growth sleeve: it is a small/mid-cap (~£1bn) UK industrial with thin, partly stale analyst coverage, a genuine but still-minority AI growth engine (~19% of group revenue), and legacy segments large enough to meaningfully dilute the headline story — enough real upside to track closely, not enough resolved risk for a Core or Anchor sizing today. TEI capital is currently better deployed in higher-conviction ideas already held in the fund - this is a capital-allocation call, not a rejection of the thesis. Monitor: the FTSE Russell September 2026 index review, and the 16 November 2026 half-year results. Next formal watchlist review: whichever of those two comes first.
