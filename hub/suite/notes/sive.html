Start Here: Why a Laser Company Is an AI Investment

The connection between a small Swedish semiconductor company and the global artificial intelligence buildout is not obvious at first glance. To understand it, you need to understand a physical problem that every hyperscale data centre in the world is running into right now - and why the solution requires an ingredient that almost nobody can make.

The Problem

AI Is Hitting a Physical Wall - and the Wall Is Made of Copper

When you run an AI model - when ChatGPT answers a question, when Netflix recommends a show, when a self-driving car processes a camera feed - that calculation happens inside a data centre where thousands of specialised chips (GPUs) are working together simultaneously. Those chips need to communicate with each other constantly, at extraordinary speed.

Today, most of that communication happens over copper wires. Copper has been the backbone of electronic communication for 150 years. But as AI clusters grow larger and the data volumes grow more extreme, copper is reaching its physical limits. As data rates push toward 800 gigabits per second and beyond, copper cables generate too much heat, consume too much power, introduce too much signal degradation, and simply cannot carry the bandwidth that modern AI workloads demand.

According to IEEE research, dielectric loss in copper interconnects at high speeds has hit what engineers call a "physical brick wall." NVIDIA itself has publicly acknowledged that interconnect - the wiring between chips - is now the primary bottleneck in AI cluster performance, not the chips themselves.

Think of it this way: Imagine trying to supply water to a city of one million people through pipes designed for a village of 1,000. You can make the pipes slightly wider, you can increase the pressure, but eventually the fundamental physics of the pipe material stops you. That is what is happening to copper in AI data centres. The solution is to rebuild the pipes - using light instead of electricity.

The Solution

Why Light Beats Electricity for Moving Data

Light travels at the speed of light. It does not generate heat the way electrical current does. It is immune to electromagnetic interference. It can carry vastly more data per unit of energy than a copper wire. And in a world where AI data centres are consuming gigawatts of power, reducing the energy cost of data transmission from chip to chip is not a marginal improvement - it is an architectural imperative.

The technology that does this is called photonics - using light (photons) rather than electricity (electrons) to transmit data. The concept has existed for decades: it is why the internet runs on fibre-optic cables rather than copper telephone lines. What is new in 2026 is that photonics is moving from the connections between data centres (long-haul fibre) to the connections inside data centres - and ultimately, directly onto the chip packages themselves.

Think of it this way: The internet runs on light - fibre-optic cables carry data as pulses of laser light across oceans and continents. Now imagine bringing that same principle inside a single data centre rack, then inside a single server, then directly onto the chip itself. That is the direction AI infrastructure is moving. Every step closer to the chip reduces power consumption, increases speed, and decreases latency. The technology that enables this shift is co-packaged optics.

The Architecture

What Is Co-Packaged Optics - and Why Does It Require a Laser?

Co-Packaged Optics (CPO) is the technology of integrating an optical engine - the component that converts electrical signals into light and back again - directly alongside the compute chip in a single package. Instead of sending electrical signals from the chip to a separate pluggable transceiver module (a small device that slots into a port on a switch), the optical conversion happens right next to the chip, massively reducing the distance the electrical signal has to travel and therefore the power it consumes.

Broadcom and NVIDIA have already introduced CPO for data centre switches. NVIDIA has stated that CPO for scale-up interconnect within AI clusters will begin in 2027-2028. LightCounting forecasts that the laser and photonics market for optical transceivers will grow from $2.4 billion in 2023 to $5.9 billion by 2029. Every major hyperscaler - Microsoft, Meta, Google, Amazon - is actively deploying CPO in their next-generation infrastructure.

But here is the critical detail: silicon cannot generate light. Silicon photonics - the dominant platform for CPO - is excellent at modulating and routing light, but it cannot produce a laser beam. Every CPO system needs an External Light Source (ELS) - an independent laser that provides the continuous beam of light that the silicon photonic chip then manipulates. Without the laser, the CPO system does not work. The laser is not optional, not substitutable, and not something that can be made from silicon.

Think of it this way: A fibre-optic cable needs a source of light at one end. Without a laser to generate that light, the cable is just glass. Silicon photonics is the cable - brilliant at carrying and manipulating light, but it cannot create light from nothing. The external laser source is the sun that powers the whole system. Every CPO deployment needs one.

The Chokepoint

Why InP Lasers Are Scarce - and Why That Makes Sivers Valuable

The lasers required for CPO External Light Sources must be made from Indium Phosphide (InP) - a compound semiconductor that can generate light efficiently at the wavelengths required for data centre optical communication. InP is not silicon. It cannot be made in standard silicon fabs. It requires completely different manufacturing equipment, different processes, and expertise that has taken decades to develop.

The manufacturing of InP laser arrays is extraordinarily difficult. Wafer yields are typically below 30% - meaning that for every 100 wafers that go into a production line, fewer than 30 emerge with usable product. Only a handful of companies in the world have the capability to manufacture InP laser arrays at anything approaching commercial scale: Lumentum, Coherent, Sumitomo Electric, Furukawa Electric, Win Semiconductor - and Sivers Semiconductors.

In Q1 2026, the CEO of IntelliEPI - one of the world's leading InP wafer manufacturers - publicly stated that "InP supply is tight and represents a bottleneck for the entire AI infrastructure." Supply of CW (continuous-wave) lasers at established Japanese suppliers (Sumitomo, Furukawa) and at Win Semiconductor is already highly saturated due to existing NVIDIA-related orders. As CPO deployment accelerates toward 2027-2028, the gap between available InP laser supply and hyperscaler demand will widen materially.

Sivers Semiconductors sits directly at this chokepoint. It manufactures InP laser arrays at its proprietary Glasgow fab and through Win Semiconductor outsourcing. It has active design-in relationships with the companies building the CPO modules that will go into the next generation of AI data centres. And it is doing so at a market cap that, as recently as March 2026, was below $200 million - smaller than the annual IP budget of some of its potential customers.

⛏️  Layer 1 - Upstream Raw Material

InP Substrates: The Foundation Everything Else Depends On

Indium Phosphide (InP) wafers are the crystal substrate on which laser structures are grown. Manufacturing InP at commercial quality requires specialised crystal growth equipment, extreme purity controls, and decades of process knowledge. The raw materials - indium and phosphorus - are themselves relatively scarce, and China controls a significant portion of global indium production. In Q1 2026, the CEO of IntelliEPI publicly confirmed that InP supply represents a bottleneck for the entire AI infrastructure build-out - validating a prediction Serenity (@aleabitoreddit) had made a year earlier. This constraint flows directly downstream: limited InP substrate supply limits InP laser production, which limits CPO deployment, which limits AI cluster performance.

💡  Layer 2 - THE CHOKEPOINT - TEI Holding

InP Laser Arrays: The Light Source CPO Cannot Live Without

Silicon photonics is brilliant at manipulating light - routing, splitting, modulating - but it cannot generate light from nothing. Every co-packaged optics system requires an External Light Source (ELS): a continuous-wave (CW) InP laser providing a stable beam for the silicon photonic chip to work with. Without the InP laser, there is no CPO. Without CPO, AI clusters cannot scale beyond the copper interconnect wall. Manufacturing InP laser arrays is extraordinarily difficult: wafer yields are sub-30%, specialist InP fabs are extremely rare, and the process requires decades of accumulated expertise. Sivers Semiconductors is one of the handful of companies globally capable of manufacturing InP laser arrays at commercial scale. Its fab-lite model - combining a proprietary Glasgow InP fab with WIN Semiconductor outsourcing - gives it a dual-path manufacturing architecture that maximises both quality control and volume flexibility.

This packaging involves optical alignment, thermal management, electrical connections, and in many cases combining multiple laser wavelengths (using CWDM or DWDM multiplexing) into a single output. The companies at Layer 3 are Sivers' direct commercial customers for Photonics revenue - each ELS module incorporates Sivers laser arrays as the critical light-generating component. 🧲  Layer 4 - The Optical Engine

Silicon Photonics / CPO: The Platform That Needs Sivers' Light

Silicon photonics platforms integrate optical waveguides, modulators, splitters, and detectors onto standard silicon using CMOS-compatible processes - making them manufacturable at semiconductor scale. Co-packaged optics (CPO) takes these silicon photonic components and integrates them directly into the same package as the compute or switching chip, eliminating the long electrical path to an external pluggable transceiver. The result: dramatically lower power consumption, higher bandwidth, and lower latency. Broadcom and NVIDIA have already deployed CPO in production switches. NVIDIA plans CPO for GPU-to-GPU scale-up interconnect in 2027-2028. The GlobalFoundries partnership (June 2, 2026) directly connects Sivers' laser arrays into GF's SCALE CPO platform - the clearest commercial proof that Layer 4 players want Sivers' technology.

What Sivers Semiconductors Actually Is

Sivers Semiconductors AB (Nasdaq Stockholm: SIVE) is a Swedish semiconductor company founded in 1951 and headquartered in Kista, north of Stockholm. It operates across two distinct business segments - Photonics and Wireless - which are in very different stages of their commercial trajectories.

Segment 1: Wireless (Current Revenue Base - ~70% of FY2025 Revenue)

The Wireless segment generates the majority of current revenue through RF beamformer chips and mmWave transceivers used in satellite communications (SATCOM), 5G infrastructure, radar, and defence applications. The flagship commercial relationship is with ALL.SPACE, a next-generation SATCOM terminal manufacturer that uses approximately 1,800 Sivers chips per terminal, representing roughly $9,000 of Sivers content per unit. This segment generated SEK 211.7M (~$22M) in FY2025 and provides the near-term revenue base while the Photonics segment scales.

Segment 2: Photonics (The Growth Engine - The Reason to Own SIVE)

The Photonics segment is why Sivers exists as an investment thesis. It develops and manufactures indium phosphide (InP) laser arrays and photonic chips for co-packaged optics, silicon photonics, LiDAR, and AI data centre applications. In Q4 2025, the Photonics segment achieved its first positive quarterly EBITDA of SEK +16.0M (~$1.7M). It is still small relative to the Wireless segment but is growing significantly faster and carries the long-term value of the company.

The Wireless segment is not a distraction - it is the financial bridge. It provides the revenue base and institutional relationships that fund photonics R&D and keep the company solvent while the CPO market matures from prototype to volume production. The key investment question for SIVE is not whether Wireless is growing - it is whether Photonics reaches volume revenue before the company requires further dilutive capital raises. The SEK 700M (~$67M) raise of July 2026 has extended that runway considerably.

Physical AI: The Wave After Data Centres

The AI buildout has two phases. The first - currently underway - is the data centre phase: building the GPU clusters that train and run AI models. This is where the CPO story lives. The second phase - beginning now and accelerating hard through 2027-2030 - is Physical AI: taking the intelligence built in those data centres and embedding it in the physical world. Humanoid robots, autonomous vehicles, factory automation systems, smart infrastructure. Every one of these applications requires one thing that data centres do not: the ability to see the real world in three dimensions. That requires LiDAR. And LiDAR requires InP lasers.

What Is Physical AI?

AI Leaving the Data Centre and Entering the Real World

Jensen Huang declared at CES 2025 that physical AI had reached its "ChatGPT moment" - the inflection point where a technology moves from research labs to commercial deployment at scale. By 2026, that prediction is proving accurate. Boston Dynamics' Atlas is sequencing car parts at Hyundai's Georgia Metaplant. Agility Robotics' Digit has moved 100,000+ totes at GXO warehouses - and is a TEI Growth Fund holding (CCXI/AGLT). Tesla produces Optimus for internal use at Fremont. Figure AI's robots are operating at BMW Spartanburg.

The physical AI market was valued at $81.4 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $1.1 trillion by 2035 at a 32.53% CAGR. The humanoid robot segment alone is expected to reach $38 billion by 2035 (Goldman Sachs), with cumulative investment surpassing $50 billion by 2030. This is not a niche market - it is the next generation of the AI investment cycle.

Think of it this way: The data centre AI phase is building the brain. The Physical AI phase is giving that brain a body. The data centre uses CPO to connect thousands of chips at the speed of light. The robot uses LiDAR to perceive the physical world in three dimensions. Both require the same core ingredient from the same handful of companies: InP lasers. Sivers is one of them.

Why Humanoid Robots Need LiDAR - and Why LiDAR Needs Sivers

Every commercially deployed humanoid robot uses LiDAR as a core sensing modality. Boston Dynamics' Atlas has a 360-degree sensor head combining LiDAR, stereo cameras, RGB, and depth sensors. Agility Robotics' Digit uses LiDAR and depth cameras to navigate warehouse environments. LiDAR is not optional - it is the technology that allows a robot moving at speed to map its environment in real time, avoid collisions, and interact safely with human co-workers.

The specific type of LiDAR gaining traction for Physical AI applications is FMCW (Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave) LiDAR - the same technology used by Aeva Technologies. Unlike conventional LiDAR which only measures distance, FMCW LiDAR simultaneously measures both distance and velocity per point - enabling a robot or vehicle to know not just where an object is, but how fast it is moving, in a single measurement. This is critical for safe human-robot collaboration in warehouse and factory environments. FMCW LiDAR requires a coherent CW (continuous-wave) laser source - precisely the type of InP laser that Sivers manufactures.
