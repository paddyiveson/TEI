The difference between a good company and a good investment

Two investors can buy shares in the same company and end up with completely different results, because of the price each one paid. That price is the difference between a good company and a good investment.

A house is worth what it is worth. A stunning house in a great location can still be a bad purchase, if someone pays far more for it than it is actually worth. An ordinary house in a quiet street can turn out to be a brilliant purchase, bought at the right price. Companies work the same way.

A company is the actual business: its products, its customers, its profits. The price is something else entirely. It is simply what other investors are currently willing to pay for a small slice of that business. A wonderful company and a wonderful investment are not automatically the same thing, because one is about the business, and the other is about the price paid for it.

Price is what you pay. Value is what you get.Warren Buffett

One simple way to see the price being paid is to compare the share price with the company's profit. If a company earns £5 a share and its shares cost £75, an investor is paying £75 for that £5 of profit: fifteen years of it, at today's rate. Pay £150 for the same £5 of profit, and it becomes thirty years of it.

This multiple is a starting question, not a verdict. A lower multiple is not automatically a bargain: sometimes the market has already spotted a real problem the price correctly reflects. A higher multiple is not automatically overpriced either, because part of it can be the market paying for real growth the company has not delivered yet. Reading a valuation properly goes deeper than this single number.

The gap between the price paid and what the business is actually worth is called a margin of safety. A bigger gap leaves more room for being wrong about the future, because no one can predict a company's profits with certainty. Even a wide gap cannot fully protect against a business turning out to be worth less than assumed.

Some investors look specifically for mispriced companies: businesses the market has not yet valued correctly. A wide enough gap can create an asymmetric opportunity, with limited downside if wrong and a larger gain if right. This is how value investors often frame the hunt for one; Mohnish Pabrai calls it "heads I win, tails I don't lose much." A genuine mispricing is hard to spot, and what looks mispriced can simply be priced correctly for a reason not yet visible.

Put business quality and price paid on the same picture, and four outcomes appear.

What this does not say. None of this says whether any specific company today is cheap, expensive or fairly priced. It shows two separate questions worth asking about any business: is this a good company, and is this a good price for it. Knowing the mechanism behind that question is not the same as having the answer to either one.

Summary

01

Quality and price are separate questions.

One is about the company; the other is about what investors are currently willing to pay for a piece of it.

02

The multiple is a price tag, not a verdict.

The same profit can be bought at a low multiple or a high one, and the size of that tag changes the outcome on its own.

03

A margin of safety is room to be wrong.

A wider gap between price and worth allows more room for error, which is why the price paid matters as much as the business bought.
