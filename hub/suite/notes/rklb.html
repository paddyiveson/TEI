Start Here: What Is Rocket Lab, Today?

Rocket Lab (NASDAQ: RKLB) started as a small-satellite launch company and has spent the past few years turning itself into something broader: a company that builds the rocket, builds much of the satellite that flies on it, and — if its pending acquisition of Iridium closes — will also operate a satellite network that generates recurring subscription revenue. Founded by New Zealand engineer Peter Beck, who remains CEO, Rocket Lab flew its first orbital mission in 2018 and went public via SPAC in 2021.

Two rockets matter here. Electron is the small launcher that made Rocket Lab's name: proven, reliable, and flying at record cadence. Neutron is the much larger, partially reusable rocket still in development that Rocket Lab needs in order to compete for the medium-lift missions SpaceX's Falcon 9 currently dominates — and to unlock the U.S. military's largest launch contracts. Rocket Lab's other companies in this batch put it plainly: Rocket Lab is often framed as the more practical, smaller-scale way to get thematic exposure to the space economy relative to SpaceX's outright dominance. That framing is broadly fair, but it undersells how much Rocket Lab itself has changed in the past two months.

In Plain English

Why does one more rocket matter so much?

Electron can only lift small satellites — a few hundred kilograms. Most of the valuable, high-margin launch business (large government satellites, mega-constellations, national-security payloads) requires a rocket that can carry many tons, which is exactly the market Falcon 9 has owned for a decade. Neutron is Rocket Lab's ticket into that market. Without it, Rocket Lab stays a niche small-launch and satellite-parts supplier; with it, Rocket Lab becomes a credible second full-service launch provider behind SpaceX. That is the single biggest swing factor in this stock.

What Changed on June 29, 2026

From launch company to would-be network operator

Rocket Lab agreed to acquire Iridium Communications, the operator of a 66-satellite mission-critical communications constellation, for roughly $8 billion in cash and stock. Iridium generated $871 million in revenue last year with real, recurring cash flow — a scale of income Rocket Lab's own business has never approached. CEO Peter Beck called the combination a "fully integrated self-launching space superpower." It is also, by a wide margin, the largest and most complex thing Rocket Lab has ever attempted, layered directly on top of an already-delayed Neutron program.

The Investment Thesis: Three Legs, Two of Them Unproven

Rocket Lab's re-rating case rests on three things, only one of which is actually done.

1. Electron: a proven, cash-generating launch business

Rocket Lab flew its 93rd Electron orbital mission on August 20, 2026, with an overall mission success rate of roughly 95% since the vehicle's 2017 debut and more than 50 consecutive successful flights since its last failure in September 2023. Demand is accelerating, not plateauing: Rocket Lab signed 36 new launch contracts in Q1 2026 alone — more than the entire 21 missions it flew in all of 2025 — and in May 2026 signed the largest launch deal in company history with a confidential customer for five Neutron and three Electron missions. NASA has also selected Electron for three science missions launching in 2027. This part of the thesis is no longer speculative.

2. Space Systems: the business that quietly became the majority of revenue

Rocket Lab now builds satellite structures, solar power systems, propulsion, separation systems, and increasingly whole spacecraft for other operators and the U.S. government — and this Space Systems segment has grown from a minority into the clear majority of the company's business. It is the least glamorous, most de-risked part of the thesis, and the part actually driving the headline revenue growth today.

3. Neutron and Iridium: the two bets that would make Rocket Lab a genuinely different company

Neutron gives Rocket Lab access to the medium-lift launch market and the U.S. military's most lucrative launch contracts. Iridium gives it an already-built, cash-generating satellite network and hard-to-obtain radio spectrum. Either one alone would be a meaningful undertaking for a company Rocket Lab's size; pursuing both at once, with Neutron's timeline already under public pressure, is the crux of the risk/reward debate in this note.

Neutron: The Single Biggest Swing Factor

Neutron is an ~13-15 tonne-to-orbit, partially reusable rocket designed to land its first stage on a barge, much like SpaceX's Falcon 9. It is central to the thesis for two reasons: it is the rocket that lets Rocket Lab compete for medium-lift commercial and government payloads, and it is a prerequisite for Rocket Lab to bid on the U.S. Space Force's larger, more lucrative National Security Space Launch (NSSL) contracts.

The program has already slipped once in a serious way. On January 21, 2026, Neutron's first-stage propellant tank ruptured during hydrostatic testing, traced to a manufacturing defect in its hand-laid composite structure. That pushed the target debut from Q1 2026 to "no earlier than" Q4 2026. Rocket Lab says a replacement tank with design improvements is now in production and that future tank manufacturing is shifting to a more automated process to reduce the risk of a repeat defect.

What's genuinely unclear: On the August 10, 2026 Q2 earnings call, CEO Peter Beck acknowledged that "the window for an end-of-year launch is narrowing," while maintaining that current tank production still lines up with a Q4 2026 delivery to the pad. Multiple outlets, including Bloomberg, reported this as the company effectively warning the maiden flight could slip into 2027. No firm date exists as of this note, and a rocket's very first orbital attempt carries meaningful chance of failure or further delay even once it reaches the pad — Neutron should be treated as unproven hardware until it actually flies successfully.

The stakes of the delay go beyond schedule optics. A short-seller report from Bleecker Street Research, originally published in 2025, alleged Rocket Lab had repeatedly overstated Neutron's readiness and understated the risk of delay to its NSSL Lane 1 eligibility; a Neutron slip past 2026 would mean Rocket Lab misses roughly another year of eligibility for the most lucrative single bucket of U.S. military launch contracts, a criticism that has aged uncomfortably well given the January tank failure and August's softened guidance.

Space Systems: The Business Rocket Lab Actually Runs Today

In Q2 2026, Rocket Lab's product revenue (largely Space Systems: satellite structures, components, and full spacecraft) reached $181.3 million, up 95.6% year-over-year, while service revenue (largely Electron launches) was roughly flat at $52.7 million, up just 1.8%. Rocket Lab does not report a single clean "Launch vs. Space Systems" dollar split in its press releases, but the direction is unambiguous: as of year-end 2025, Space Systems already represented 74% of the company's $1.85 billion backlog, up from a minority share a few years earlier. The company that built its brand on Electron now earns most of its money building things that fly on other people's and its own rockets.

This shift is deliberate, not accidental. Rocket Lab acquired satellite-payload maker Geost for $275 million (closed), adding electro-optical/infrared sensor manufacturing and positioning the company as what it calls a "disruptive prime" for national-security payloads. In August 2026 it delivered the first satellites under a $143 million contract with MDA Space. Rocket Lab has also pursued laser-communication-terminal maker Mynaric, though the status of that deal is less definitively confirmed in public reporting than Geost's. The pattern is consistent: buy the pieces that let Rocket Lab sell a customer an entire spacecraft, not just a ride to orbit.

The Iridium Acquisition: Betting the Balance Sheet on Vertical Integration

On June 29, 2026, Rocket Lab agreed to acquire Iridium Communications for $54 per Iridium share — $27 in cash plus Rocket Lab stock at an exchange ratio subject to a collar between $67.50 and $112.50 — a deal valued at roughly $8 billion. Rocket Lab lined up a $3.6 billion bridge loan (Deutsche Bank, Wells Fargo) to fund the cash portion, plus up to $2.1 billion more to refinance Iridium's existing debt. Both boards approved the deal unanimously, Hart-Scott-Rodino antitrust clearance has since lapsed, and an S-4 registration has been filed; closing is expected in mid-2027, subject to Iridium shareholder approval and remaining regulatory sign-offs.

Why Rocket Lab wants this: Iridium brings a functioning, revenue-generating satellite constellation ($871 million in 2025 revenue) and L-band mobile-satellite spectrum that CEO Peter Beck called "almost impossible to get" — weather-resistant communications used by pilots, mariners, first responders, and remote infrastructure operators. Analysts framed the strategic logic bluntly: remaining a standalone launch provider is increasingly untenable when the biggest customers (SpaceX, Amazon) are themselves vertically integrated and building their own networks rather than buying launch as a service. Iridium converts Rocket Lab's revenue mix from project-based (win a contract, deliver, repeat) toward subscription-based recurring income, which is a materially different, more durable kind of business.

The sharp jump in Rocket Lab's own cash balance this quarter — from $828.7 million at year-end 2025, to $1.48 billion after Q1, to $2.13 billion after Q2 — is largely balance-sheet preparation for this deal (equity raises and financing activity ahead of the cash consideration and debt refinancing), not organic profit accumulation. Treat the cash stat as war chest, not earnings power.

What's genuinely unclear: This is the largest acquisition in Rocket Lab's history, closing a full year or more after announcement, with real execution risk in between. Analysts covering the deal flag three specific tests: securing satellite-license and market-access renewals across dozens of jurisdictions, building Iridium's next-generation replacement constellation in-house on budget (described by one analyst as "the real-life test of the entire business thesis"), and maintaining service continuity for government and commercial subscribers through the transition — all while a still-unprofitable Rocket Lab carries new acquisition debt and simultaneously tries to fly Neutron for the first time. No single piece of this is disqualifying on its own; the combination is what makes it worth tracking closely rather than assuming success.

Financial Position: Record Growth, Still Unprofitable

Rocket Lab posted record Q2 2026 revenue of $234.1 million, up 62% year-over-year and ahead of the roughly $232 million analysts expected. GAAP gross margin was 36.1%. Net loss narrowed to $49.3 million from $66.4 million a year earlier, though GAAP EPS of -$0.08 missed the -$0.06 consensus estimate. Backlog reached a record $2.36 billion, up 137% year-over-year. For Q3 2026, Rocket Lab guided to revenue of $250-265 million (another record) with non-GAAP gross margin of 35-37% and an adjusted EBITDA loss narrowing to $17-23 million.

The growth and narrowing losses are real, and Q3 guidance implies another record quarter. But Rocket Lab is still not profitable, is about to add $3.6 billion of bridge financing on top of an already cash-consuming Neutron program, and trades at roughly 53 times trailing sales — a valuation that prices in a great deal of successful execution across two programs that have not yet been proven out.

Government & Defense: Real Wins, But SpaceX Still Wins the Big Ones

Rocket Lab has been building a genuine national-security book of business, but the highest-value defense launch awards continue to go to SpaceX. In July 2026, Rocket Lab won a record $266 million U.S. Space Force contract for up to 18 suborbital HASTE missions supporting hypersonic weapons testing out of Kodiak, Alaska. Separately, Rocket Lab joined Raytheon as a partner on the Golden Dome missile-defense program, contributing to space-based interceptor development. However, when the Space Force awarded its marquee Golden Dome Space-Based Target (SBST) launch missions in July 2026, all 18 went to SpaceX, underscoring how completely SpaceX still dominates the highest-profile, highest-value tranche of U.S. defense launch demand.

Rocket Lab has been part of the NSSL (National Security Space Launch) Lane 1 vendor pool since March 2025, alongside newer entrants like Stoke Space, but has not yet won a Lane 1 task order — those have gone predominantly to SpaceX so far, with the Space Force recently tripling Lane 1's contract capacity to $17 billion. Lane 2, which covers the largest and most valuable national-security missions, requires Neutron to be flying; Rocket Lab cannot meaningfully compete for that bucket until Neutron proves itself.

SpaceX's dominance is not really contestable at Rocket Lab's scale — it is roughly 35x Rocket Lab's market cap and has flown more than five times as many orbital missions as Rocket Lab has, with routine daily-cadence reuse. Rocket Lab's actual competitive set for capital allocation purposes is the tier below: Firefly (public, smaller, further from a reusable medium-lift vehicle) and Relativity (private, still pre-launch on Terran R). Within that tier, Rocket Lab is the only one with a proven, currently-flying rocket, a real vertically-integrated spacecraft business, and (pending) an operating satellite network — but it is also the only one carrying an $8 billion acquisition on top of an unflown next-generation rocket.

Key Risks

Neutron timeline risk ~53x price-to-sales $3.6B Iridium bridge loan Still unprofitable Golden Dome SBST lost to SpaceX Short-seller scrutiny

Neutron is the biggest single risk in this note. A first-stage tank rupture in January 2026 already pushed the debut from Q1 to Q4 2026, and management's own language on the August earnings call ("the window is narrowing") plus subsequent reporting of possible 2027 slippage suggests real uncertainty remains. A rocket's first orbital attempt also carries inherent failure risk even once it reaches the pad. Every quarter Neutron doesn't fly is a quarter Rocket Lab can't compete for NSSL Lane 2 or medium-lift commercial contracts.

The valuation prices in a lot of things going right at once. At roughly 53 times trailing sales against a broader tech-sector average closer to 7x, RKLB is priced for continued 60%+ revenue growth, a successful Neutron debut, and a clean Iridium integration — not for any one of those slipping meaningfully.

The Iridium deal adds real leverage and integration risk on top of an already-stretched execution plate. A $3.6 billion bridge loan plus up to $2.1 billion to refinance Iridium's debt is substantial for a company still posting quarterly net losses. Analysts covering the deal have explicitly called the in-house build of Iridium's next-generation constellation "the real-life test of the entire business thesis." The deal doesn't close until mid-2027, leaving over a year of execution risk, regulatory approvals across "dozens of jurisdictions," and integration planning before it's final.

SpaceX's dominance in the highest-value defense contracts hasn't budged. Rocket Lab is winning real, growing government business — the $266M Space Force HASTE award, the Raytheon Golden Dome interceptor partnership — but when the Space Force's marquee Golden Dome SBST launch missions were awarded, all 18 went to SpaceX. Rocket Lab remains a credible secondary player in national-security space, not yet a peer competitor to SpaceX for the largest contracts.

A short-seller thesis on Neutron has partly aged well. Bleecker Street Research's 2025 report alleging Rocket Lab overstated Neutron's readiness looks more credible in hindsight given the January 2026 tank failure and the company's softened August 2026 guidance — a reminder to treat management's launch-date commentary with some skepticism until hardware is actually on the pad.

This is a stock with a proven, growing core business (Electron and Space Systems) trading at a valuation that assumes two much larger, unproven bets (Neutron, Iridium) both succeed roughly on schedule. Position sizing should assume sharp moves around every Neutron milestone, every Iridium regulatory update, and every earnings print between now and mid-2027.

What would move this from watchlist to portfolio consideration: (1) a successful Neutron maiden orbital launch — the single clearest de-risking event available; (2) confirmation the Iridium deal is progressing to close on its announced terms, without renegotiated economics or a surprise dilutive capital raise; (3) a first NSSL Lane 1 or Lane 2 task-order win validating that U.S. government launch demand is genuinely broadening beyond SpaceX; or (4) the valuation compressing toward a price-to-sales multiple more consistent with realized (rather than promised) growth, without a corresponding deterioration in the underlying thesis. Without at least one of these, the current risk/reward does not justify adding it to the fund over existing holdings.

Bottom Line: A Proven Rocket Company Making Two Unproven Bets At Once

Rocket Lab's core business is no longer speculative. Electron flies reliably and at record cadence, and Space Systems has grown into the majority of company revenue on the back of real contracts (MDA Space, Geost's national-security payloads, a record backlog). What is speculative is everything layered on top: Neutron, which needs to actually reach orbit successfully after already slipping once, and the $8 billion Iridium acquisition, which needs to close on schedule in mid-2027 and then integrate cleanly. Both are individually plausible. Betting on both landing roughly together, at 53 times sales, is a materially higher-risk proposition than the underlying launch-and-manufacturing business alone would justify.

Near term: The next few quarters are about watching whether Neutron's Q4 2026 target holds, whether the Iridium deal continues to clear regulatory and shareholder hurdles without surprises, and whether Q3 guidance ($250-265M revenue) is met. None of these has a fixed, guaranteed outcome as of this note.

Long term: if Neutron flies successfully and scales, and Iridium integrates cleanly, Rocket Lab becomes a genuinely diversified space company — launch, spacecraft manufacturing, and an operating satellite network with recurring revenue — a much more resilient business than a pure launch play, and one of very few credible alternatives to SpaceX at any meaningful scale.

TEI positioning: Watchlist - not a current Growth Fund holding. If added, this would sit at Tail tier within the Growth Fund sleeve: the core launch-and-manufacturing business is real, but the stock is priced for near-flawless execution on two large, unproven bets simultaneously, which is too much stacked, binary risk for a Core or Anchor-sized position — while the proven Electron/Space Systems foundation and genuine long-term upside if Neutron and Iridium both land make it more than a pass. TEI capital is currently better deployed in higher-conviction ideas already held in the fund - this is a capital-allocation call, not a rejection of the thesis. Monitor: Neutron's maiden launch attempt (targeted Q4 2026), the Iridium deal's progress toward its mid-2027 close, and any NSSL Lane 1 or Lane 2 task-order award. Next review: Q3 2026 earnings, or immediately upon a Neutron launch attempt, whichever comes first.
