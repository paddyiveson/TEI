The ONDS Disconnect: a defence prime in the making, priced like a problem

Ondas has never had more good news, yet the shares trade at well under half their January high. What Ondas has become, what shareholders paid for it, and what would make the price follow the business.

Disclosure: Ondas shares are held in the TEI Growth Portfolio and by the founder of TEI. This article explains the company. It is not a recommendation to buy, sell or hold.

The story in 30 seconds

Ondas has bought its way from a small drone company to something close to a defence prime: one supplier covering every layer of drone warfare, from ground robots to balloons in the stratosphere, joined up by AI software built with Palantir. The business is real and growing fast. Revenue is guided to rise about tenfold this year, and management expects to leave 2026 at a $1bn annual run rate.

The share price has not followed, because shareholders paid for that growth with new shares, about six times as many as at the end of 2024. Heavy short selling, a wall of warrants and shares about to become sellable mean rallies get sold into. New orders no longer move the price. Proof would: revenue landing as guided, losses shrinking on schedule and a share count that stops climbing. If that proof arrives, the same forces could work the other way. The next test is the Q3 results on 12 November.

Read just this and you have the story. Each chapter below explains one part, with the fine detail folded away.

01 · The paradox

The news kept getting better. The price did not.

Most of 2026 has been one positive headline after another, while the shares drifted lower. Understanding why is the key to this whole article.

The shares closed at a record $15.28 on 12 January. On 20 October they closed at $6.10: about 60% below the high and down 37.5% this year. Over the same months Ondas announced a Palantir partnership, beat its own forecasts, closed its largest acquisition, reported a record quarter and won more than $270M of new orders.

ONDS share price in 2026, with the good news placed on the line

    The detail: what the price is telling us

    The record high came in the same week Ondas announced a $1bn share and warrant raise. Since then the pattern has repeated: good news lifts the price for a day or two, then it drifts back.

    That is unusual. A company raising its revenue target this sharply would normally be rewarded. Three forces explain most of it: dilution, doubt about whether more than 20 acquired companies can work as one and make money, and selling pressure from short sellers and warrant holders. Chapter 05 takes the problems in turn; chapter 06 sets out the other side.

    02 · What Ondas is now

    From drone maker to defence prime

    We call it Call of Duty in a can: everything a modern military needs for drone warfare, spotting, watching, stopping and striking, from one supplier and joined up by one piece of software.

    A defence prime is a company that signs contracts directly with governments and runs whole programmes, rather than selling parts to someone who does. Two years ago Ondas sold automated drones to industrial customers. Today it covers every layer of drone warfare and, through Mistral, holds a prime contractor position with the US Army.

    A military customer can buy the whole kit from one supplier instead of a dozen products from a dozen companies. That is the bet. It has not yet been proven at scale.

    The Ondas platform, layer by layer
    Every layer of drone warfare, from the ground to the edge of space, and every piece reporting to the same software brain.
    Route to customers: Mistral makes Ondas a prime contractor to the US Army.

    Why being a prime matters

    Most small defence companies sell to the primes. Mistral changed that for Ondas. It is a prime contractor on a $982M US Army programme. More than $240M has been awarded so far, which leaves about $740M still to be ordered over the life of the programme. Those future orders depend on Army decisions and budgets, and are not guaranteed.

    The rest of the platform was bought, fast. Ondas has acquired more than 20 companies since late 2025, many of them paid for partly in new shares. Management says the pace of deals will now slow.

    The detail: every company and where it sits
    • Ondas Sentinel: DZYNE and World View. Long-endurance aircraft, stratospheric balloons, the Dronebuster counter-drone tool and strike systems. DZYNE alone is expected to bring about $191M of revenue in 2026.
    • Ondas Autonomous Systems: American Robotics and Airobotics (Optimus), Iron Drone, Sentrycs, Apeiro Motion and Roboteam.
    • Other group companies: Mistral, Omnisys (battlefield orchestration software), BIRD Aerosystems, Rotron, SPO (majority stake), Insight and Indo. Since July: Cyberhawk, High Point, GATE and Bron, plus three smaller deals in September.

    Potential programmes: Ondas cites a NATO programme worth up to $300M and a border programme worth up to $100M. These are company-stated potential values, not orders.

    In its accounts, Ondas valued Mistral's programme relationships at $86.3M.

    03 · The software

    What takes Ondas to the next level

    Hardware is paid for once. Software is paid for again and again, as threats change and as customers add drones and missions.

    Building the kit is only half the job. Back to Call of Duty: the hardware is the loadout; the software is what lets every piece of kit play as one team. Without it, Ondas is a box of parts.

    That is why the Palantir partnership matters. Palantir's software is built to pull data from many different systems into one picture and act on it, which is exactly the job of joining up more than 20 acquired companies.

    There is early proof. SkyWeaver, the command system Ondas is building with Palantir, flew AI aboard a World View balloon on 5 August, so it is past the drawing board. Cyberhawk, which sells drone inspection on subscription, shows the model working: existing customers spent 112% of what they spent the year before.

    The detail: the programmes, and what is still missing

    The Palantir partnership has three programmes: Warp Speed (manufacturing and readiness), AI Flight Director (mission planning) and SkyWeaver (intelligence processed on the vehicle itself, so it works when the connection is poor).

    Regna AI, launched on 12 October and wholly owned by Ondas for now, builds specialist AI models for military missions that governments can train and run on infrastructure they control. It has no outside customer or contract value yet, so the market has nothing to price.

    Omnisys, bought in May, adds battlefield orchestration software. Cyberhawk's figures are management estimates.

    04 · The growth

    Revenue guided to rise about tenfold in a year

    This is the part the bulls are right about. The business is growing far faster than almost any listed defence company.

    Revenue was $50.7M in 2025. The target for 2026 is $525M to $550M. Q1 and Q2 are done. Q3 is guided at $140M to $155M, and the full-year target implies about $256M in Q4, close to the analyst average of about $255M. Leave 2026 at that pace and Ondas is running at about $1bn a year, which is management's stated aim.

    Much of the jump comes from acquisitions. Counting acquired businesses in both years, Q2 revenue grew about 85%. That is the figure that shows whether the businesses are growing on their own, and it is still strong.

    Beyond signed orders, Ondas reports a two-year pipeline of opportunities that grew from $4bn in May to more than $11bn in August. Outside forecasts point the same way, and the US budget request for next year puts about $74bn towards drones and counter-drone systems.

    The detail: organic growth and what a pipeline is
    • Backlog, orders signed but not yet delivered, was $757M on a pro forma basis at the end of June, up 33% from Q1. Ondas has announced more than $270M of new orders since.
    • A pipeline is opportunities, not orders. Only some of it will turn into revenue.
    • Ondas puts its own addressable market at about $130bn. That figure is the company's.
    • The FY27 US defence budget request is $1.5tn. It is a request, not yet approved.

    Execution

    The worry. Buying companies is the easy part. Joining them up, keeping their people and selling them as one platform is hard, and it is unproven here. The strategy is also closely tied to one person, the chief executive, Eric Brock.

    07 · The multiple reset

    What you are paying now

    The valuation is no longer the main debate. The revenue ramp is close to being priced in. The question is whether it becomes profit.

    At the January high, Ondas was valued at roughly 30 times the 2026 revenue it was then guiding, $170M to $180M. Today it is valued at about 5 times a guide that has since tripled.

    The price fell about 60% while expectations rose about threefold. The market has stopped paying for the story in advance. That cuts both ways: the bar is lower, but the market is waiting to see profit before it pays more.

    The average analyst price target was about $18.50 on 21 October 2026, across 8 analysts (Bigdata.com). Targets are opinions about the future and are often wrong.

    The detail: how to read these numbers
    • Enterprise value is market value of about $3.55bn less our estimate of year-end net cash of about $775M: about $2.8bn.
    • It leaves out future shares from warrants and earn-outs, which would make the business look dearer.
    • Large defence companies usually trade at a small multiple of sales because they grow slowly and steadily.
    • A company can reach $1bn of revenue and still disappoint shareholders if margins stay thin or the share count keeps rising.

    Key takeaways

    Five things to remember

    1. GrowthRevenue is guided to rise about tenfold this year, with management aiming to leave 2026 at about $1bn a year.
    2. DilutionShareholders paid with about six times as many shares as at the end of 2024, though revenue per share has grown faster.
    3. Selling pressureShort interest of around 40%, warrants at $20 and $28, and the January unlock lean on every rally.
    4. CashA large cash pile and almost no debt mean Ondas does not need to raise money to keep going.
    5. ProofThe price is waiting for revenue to land, losses to shrink on schedule and the share count to stop rising. The first test is 12 November.

    What this does not say

    It does not say Ondas is a good or bad investment, that the price will rise after the results, or that you should buy, sell or hold. It explains why the price and the business have parted, and what would bring them back together.

    The bottom line

    The business has run ahead of the share price

    Ondas has built something real, quickly, and paid for it with its shareholders' ownership. The market has stopped rewarding promises and orders. It is waiting for proof: revenue landing as guided, losses shrinking on the published dates, and a share count that stops growing.

    If that proof arrives, short selling and the warrant ceiling could work the other way. If it does not, dilution and selling pressure are likely to keep the shares where they are. Watch the results, not the headlines.

    In Call of Duty terms: the business has levelled up. The chart has not caught up.
