Start Here: What Is Grab Holdings?

Grab (NASDAQ: GRAB) is Southeast Asia’s largest “superapp” – a single app bundling deliveries (food and grocery), mobility (ride-hailing), and financial services (digital banking, lending, payments, insurance) across eight countries: Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam, the Philippines, Cambodia, and Myanmar, serving over 900 cities. It started life in 2012 as MyTeksi, a Malaysian taxi-hailing app, and came public on NASDAQ via a 2021 SPAC merger with Altimeter Growth Corp. Co-founder Anthony Tan remains CEO.

Scale is Grab’s core asset: 54 million monthly transacting users (a record) generated $6.5 billion of on-demand gross merchandise value in Q2 2026 alone, and Grab holds roughly a 55% share of ASEAN food delivery on the most recent industry survey – more than double its nearest rival. After 13 years of losses, Q2 2026 marked Grab’s second consecutive quarter of GAAP profitability, with management raising full-year guidance on the back of it.

In Plain English

Why does a ride-hailing app need a bank?

Millions of Grab’s drivers and merchants are gig workers with thin or no credit history – the kind of customer a traditional Southeast Asian bank often won’t lend to. Grab already has years of their transaction and behavioural data, so it built its own digital banks (GXS Bank in Singapore, GXBank in Malaysia, and a stake in Superbank in Indonesia) to underwrite loans and take deposits directly inside the app. It is Grab’s smallest and most loss-making segment today, but also its fastest-growing – and the one most likely to determine whether Grab becomes a true financial platform or stays a transport-and-food app with a banking side project.

What Changed Recently

A merger dies again, and Uber’s board seat goes with it

Long-running, on-and-off merger talks between Grab and Indonesian rival GoTo (Gojek/Tokopedia) were reportedly called off again in 2026, complicated by the stakes held by Indonesian state-linked investors. Separately, Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi stepped down from Grab’s board in July 2026 (Uber keeps its roughly 13.5% economic stake), around the same time Uber confirmed it is pursuing a roughly $15 billion bid for Delivery Hero – a deal that could hand Uber Foodpanda’s Southeast Asian assets and test the non-compete pact that has kept Uber out of the region since it sold its SE Asia business to Grab in 2018. None of this shows up in the earnings; all of it has shown up in the share price.

The Investment Thesis: A Profitable Platform the Market Is Discounting

Grab’s bull case rests less on a single catalyst than on a business that has finally started converting scale into cash, at a valuation that has not caught up.

1. The profitability inflection is real, not promotional

Q2 2026 revenue grew 22% year-over-year to $997 million, adjusted EBITDA grew 54% to $168 million, and GAAP net income hit $235 million – up from just $20 million a year earlier. Management raised full-year 2026 guidance to $4.10–4.15 billion in revenue and $720–740 million in adjusted EBITDA on the back of it, and backed conviction with a new $750 million share buyback authorisation.

2. Financial services is the highest-growth, highest-optionality segment

Financial services revenue grew 59% year-over-year to $134 million in Q2, with the gross loan portfolio up 197% to $2.3 billion and deposits at $2.5 billion across Grab’s three digital banks. It is still segment-EBITDA-negative, but the loss narrowed $11 million year-over-year – the segment furthest from maturity, and the one with the most room to re-rate the stock if it reaches breakeven.

3. Balance sheet gives real optionality

$7.4 billion of gross cash liquidity ($5.4 billion net) and $450 million of trailing free cash flow give Grab room to keep buying back stock, fund the newly-announced Taiwan expansion (its first market outside Southeast Asia), and absorb a GoTo deal or further digital-bank capital calls without needing to raise external capital.

The Business Mix: Three Segments, One App

Digital Banking: The Slowest-Burning Fire

Grab’s financial-services arm now runs three licensed digital banks: GXS Bank in Singapore (with Singtel), GXBank in Malaysia, and a minority stake in Superbank in Indonesia. Together they hold $2.5 billion in deposits and a $2.3 billion loan book that has nearly tripled year-over-year, built around lending to gig workers, sole proprietors, and other customers traditional banks tend to pass on.

Financial Position: Cash-Rich, Now Genuinely Profitable

Grab ended Q2 2026 with $7.4 billion in gross cash liquidity ($5.4 billion net of debt) and $450 million of trailing-twelve-month adjusted free cash flow. Unlike most of TEI’s watchlist growth names, cash burn is no longer the central risk – the company just authorised a new $750 million share buyback on top of its existing programme, a signal management believes the stock is undervalued relative to the cash it is now generating.

Competitive Landscape: Dominant Regionally, Vulnerable Locally

Grab is the clear #1 superapp across most of Southeast Asia, but its competitive picture is not uniform – it is winning the region-wide war on deliveries and losing a country-specific one on mobility, while a bigger structural competitor question (Uber) has just re-opened.

Vietnam is the exception to Grab’s regional lead. Xanh SM, the EV taxi arm of Vietnamese conglomerate Vingroup (billionaire Pham Nhat Vuong), has overtaken Grab in Vietnam’s ride-hailing market – holding roughly 54.5% share in Q1 2026 and extending an 18-month lead, backed by a fast-growing, state-adjacent EV manufacturer with deep local capital. It is a reminder that Grab’s SE Asia dominance is an aggregate, not a guarantee in every single market.

The Uber wildcard. Uber owns roughly 13.5% of Grab under a non-compete struck when it sold its SE Asia business to Grab in 2018. Uber is now pursuing a roughly $15 billion acquisition of Delivery Hero that would include Foodpanda’s Southeast Asian food-delivery assets – assets Uber has reportedly kept out of a separate divestment, which analysts read as a signal it wants to keep its ASEAN options open. If Uber and Grab can’t agree on amending the non-compete, Uber may restructure or exit its Grab stake altogether. Maybank, which still rates Grab a Buy, models a more competitive scenario cutting its Grab fair-value estimate from $6.25 to $5.55 per share. This is an emerging risk, not a resolved one, as of this note.

Next formal word on GoTo merger talks: Given the on-and-off history, a credible restart (or a definitive, sourced denial) would move the stock either way.

Key Risks

Vietnam mobility share lost to Xanh SM Uber/Foodpanda competitive threat GoTo merger uncertainty Digital banking still loss-making Multi-currency EM exposure CEO insider selling

Southeast Asia currency and regulatory risk is structural, not incidental. Grab reports in USD but earns across the Singapore dollar, Indonesian rupiah, Vietnamese dong, Thai baht, Malaysian ringgit, and Philippine peso – six currencies with meaningfully different volatility and capital-control regimes. Gig-worker regulation is a live issue in several markets (Indonesia’s ojol/motorcycle-taxi rules were explicitly linked by officials to the GoTo merger question), and a U.S. senator has separately urged the FTC to scrutinise food-delivery fee disclosure practices across the industry, naming Grab alongside Uber, DoorDash, and others.

The GoTo relationship cuts both ways. A completed merger could meaningfully consolidate the region and improve unit economics for both sides; the years of on-and-off talks and the involvement of Indonesian state-linked capital (reportedly including sovereign fund Danantara) mean this is as likely to generate more headline volatility as it is to generate a deal. Investors should not underwrite a merger happening.

Insider selling and governance changes add a modest overhang. CEO Anthony Tan sold roughly 400,000 shares (~$1.56 million) in July 2026, and Uber’s CEO exiting Grab’s board the same month – while Uber pursues its own competing ASEAN options – both read as cautionary signals even though neither is disqualifying on its own.

What would move this from watchlist to portfolio consideration: (1) a confirmed positive Adjusted EBITDA quarter in Financial Services, evidencing the digital-banking bet has turned the corner; (2) resolution of the Uber/Delivery Hero non-compete question in Grab’s favour, removing the biggest emerging competitive overhang; (3) the stock re-rating toward a level that reflects the current profitability (i.e., closing a meaningful chunk of the gap to the ~$5.73 average analyst target) without giving back the fundamental improvement; or (4) a credible, sourced restart of GoTo merger talks with clearer terms than prior rounds. Without at least one of these, the current risk/reward does not justify adding it to the fund over existing holdings.

This is a profitable, cash-generative platform business trading like a much more troubled one – that gap is either an opportunity or a sign the market knows something about Vietnam, Uber, or the digital-banking timeline that this note has not fully priced in. Position any exposure assuming continued headline-driven volatility around each of the four triggers above.

Bottom Line: The Business Turned a Corner. The Stock Hasn’t Been Told Yet.

Grab’s fundamentals in Q2 2026 are the best they have been since the company went public: back-to-back GAAP-profitable quarters, 22% revenue growth, 54% Adjusted EBITDA growth, raised guidance, a $750 million buyback, and a fast-growing (if still loss-making) digital-banking arm. Against that, the stock is down roughly 30% over the past year and trades near its 52-week low – moved more by a dead-again GoTo merger, an exiting Uber board seat, Uber’s own possible ASEAN re-entry via Delivery Hero, and CEO share sales than by anything in the P&L.

Near term: the next one to two quarters should clarify whether Q2’s profitability was an inflection or a high point, and whether the Uber/Delivery Hero situation resolves into a real competitive threat or fades the way prior GoTo merger rounds have.

Long term: if Financial Services reaches breakeven, the Taiwan expansion proves the playbook travels, and Grab holds its regional lead outside Vietnam, this becomes a genuinely diversified Southeast Asian financial-and-logistics platform rather than a rides-and-food app. If Uber re-enters via Foodpanda and the GoTo relationship stays adversarial rather than combining, Grab’s moat gets tested on two fronts at once.

TEI positioning: Watchlist - not a current Growth Fund holding.
