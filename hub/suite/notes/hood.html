Start Here: What Is Robinhood Today?

Robinhood Markets (NASDAQ: HOOD) started in 2013 as a commission-free stock trading app aimed at young, first-time investors - the company that made "everyone an owner," in founder and CEO Vlad Tenev's phrase, and became a household name during the 2021 meme-stock mania. That origin story is now a small part of a much bigger picture. Robinhood management says the platform now has 13 distinct business lines each generating $100 million or more in annualized revenue - spanning options, crypto, equities, prediction markets, margin lending, cash sweep interest, Gold subscriptions, retirement accounts, a credit card, banking, wealth management, and international expansion.

The core tension for this note is simple: is that genuine diversification, or is it still fundamentally one thing - a trading app whose revenue rises and falls with how much its customers gamble on markets, crypto, and now sports and events - wearing more costumes than it used to?

In Plain English

What is a "prediction market" or "event contract"?

An event contract lets you buy a small stake that pays out if a specific real-world event happens - who wins the Super Bowl, whether the Fed cuts rates, who wins an election. It looks and feels like a bet, but it's structured and regulated as a financial derivative (a "swap") under the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), the same federal regulator that oversees oil and interest-rate futures - not as gambling, which is regulated state-by-state. Whether that federal framing holds up against state gambling law is currently being fought out in multiple courts, and it matters a lot to Robinhood: this product line generated $156 million in the second quarter of 2026 alone, up roughly tenfold year-over-year.

Why the Stock Doesn't Match the Earnings

A record quarter, and a stock still 30% off its highs

Robinhood's Q2 2026 numbers were the best in company history by revenue and net income. Yet the stock peaked near $154 in April 2026 and, even after an August 21 pop on a Bitcoin rally, sits around $108. The disconnect is the market pricing in two live risks that don't show up in a backward-looking earnings print: whether crypto-trading weakness (down sharply industrywide, not just at Robinhood) keeps dragging on transaction revenue, and whether prediction markets - now bigger than crypto as a revenue line - survive an active, unresolved legal fight with state regulators.

The Investment Thesis: Three Things Have to Keep Being True

Robinhood's re-rating from meme-stock app to serious fintech platform rests on three claims. Each has real evidence behind it; none is fully proven yet.

1. Revenue diversification is real, not just marketing

Net interest revenue - the "boring," least-volatile leg of the business, driven by margin lending and cash sweep balances - grew to $389 million in Q2 (+9% YoY, ~30% of total revenue) even as crypto trading cooled. Gold subscribers reached 4.8 million (+39% YoY, 17% of funded customers), and average revenue per user climbed 24% to $187. That is a genuinely broader base than the 2021-era Robinhood, which lived and died by options and crypto order flow.

2. Prediction markets are a real, fast-scaling product - with real legal risk attached

Through its Rothera exchange and clearinghouse (which Robinhood now owns outright, capturing the full economics per contract rather than sharing them with a partner), Robinhood has cleared more than 3.5 billion event contracts and generated $156 million in Q2 revenue from the category - roughly 10x its year-ago level, and now larger than crypto trading revenue. The FIFA World Cup drove most of that. Whether NFL football, which kicks off September 9, 2026, can sustain or grow that number through the back half of the year is the single biggest near-term swing factor for the stock - and it's happening while the product's basic legality in several states is still being litigated.

3. New verticals are expanding Robinhood beyond "trading app"

Robinhood Retirement (IRA) assets under custody reached $34.5 billion (+82% YoY), and the 2025 acquisition of RIA custodian TradePMR - now branded Robinhood Advisor Network - brings $50 billion of independent-advisor assets onto the platform. Both are genuine, meaningfully-sized wealth-management footholds. Banking, the credit card, and managed investing (Robinhood Strategies) are earlier and smaller - real, but not yet proven at scale (see below).

Business Mix: How Diversified Is Revenue, Really?

Of Robinhood's $1.31 billion in Q2 2026 revenue, transaction-based revenue - the part that rises and falls with how much customers actually trade - was still 59% of the total. Net interest revenue was 30%. Subscriptions and everything else (Gold, Trump Account services) made up the remaining 11%.

The honest read: Robinhood hasn't reduced its dependence on trading-volume swings so much as swapped which product drives them. Crypto - the volatile growth story of 2024-2025 - has cooled sharply (a sector-wide trend also visible at peers like Swissquote, not Robinhood-specific). Event contracts have stepped into exactly that role, growing explosively off a small base. That's a legitimate new growth leg, but it is not yet a lower-volatility one, and its legal footing is less settled than options or equities trading ever was.

Prediction Markets: The New Swing Factor, and the New Legal Fight

Prediction markets have gone from a novelty to one of Robinhood's largest single product lines in under a year, and the company now owns the full stack - Rothera is a CFTC-licensed exchange and clearinghouse Robinhood controls, rather than a third party it pays a cut to, which materially improves the economics of every contract traded.

The regulatory fight is real and unresolved. The CFTC maintains that event contracts are federally regulated commodities, preempting state gambling law. States disagree: New York sued rival operator Kalshi for operating without a gaming license, and Washington's Gambling Commission declared prediction markets "unauthorized," prompting Robinhood itself to file a preemptive federal lawsuit in Tacoma (March 2026) arguing federal law overrides state gambling statutes. The scorecard so far is a split decision - federal courts in New Jersey and Tennessee have sided with the prediction-market operators, state courts in Massachusetts and Ohio have sided with the states, 44 state attorneys general have jointly opposed the CFTC's claimed exclusive authority, and a Ninth Circuit ruling on the jurisdictional question is still pending with no confirmed date.

This is not a settled regulatory question with a known worst case. A product line that didn't meaningfully exist eighteen months ago is now roughly 12% of total company revenue and growing faster than almost anything else Robinhood sells - and its basic legality varies by state and is actively being litigated. Treat every court ruling and state enforcement action here as a genuine catalyst or risk event, not background noise.

Valuation: Does the Multiple Still Make Sense?

At ~$108, HOOD trades around 48x trailing earnings (some estimates put the forward multiple in the mid-50s) - rich for a brokerage, but Robinhood isn't really being valued as one anymore. Against the two most obvious comparisons, it lands in the middle: cheaper than a pure-play crypto exchange pricing in years of future growth, more expensive than a diversified legacy broker growing in the single digits.

Key Risks

Crypto-volume slump Prediction-markets legal risk Rich valuation vs. traditional brokers Wide analyst target dispersion New verticals still small

The crypto slump looks structural, not a one-quarter blip. Robinhood's crypto revenue fell 38% YoY in Q2 and volumes were down roughly 62% YoY in July. This mirrors what peers like Swissquote have reported (crypto revenue down over 66% in H1 2026), suggesting an industrywide retreat in retail crypto trading rather than a Robinhood-specific issue - which means it's largely outside management's control to fix.

Prediction markets are now too big to be a rounding error, and their legality is contested in real time. At ~12% of total revenue and Robinhood's fastest-growing transaction line, an adverse Ninth Circuit ruling or a wave of successful state enforcement actions (following the pattern of state-court wins in Massachusetts and Ohio) could force Robinhood to restrict or shut down the product in some states - directly hitting the line of business the market is currently paying up for.

The valuation leaves little room for disappointment. At roughly 48-55x earnings, HOOD already prices in continued double-digit growth across most of its 13 business lines. The stock's own 30% drawdown from its April high, and a $57-$163.60 analyst target range, suggest the market - and Wall Street itself - isn't fully confident in that outcome.

The diversification story isn't finished yet. Banking, the credit card, and managed investing are real products with genuine sign-up momentum, but each is still a low-single-digit share of revenue. If trading volumes (crypto or event contracts) disappoint simultaneously, there isn't yet enough recurring revenue behind the newer verticals to fully cushion the blow.

This is a fast-growing, genuinely improving business trading at a valuation and a stock price that already assume a lot goes right - continued net-interest and subscription growth, a soft landing in crypto, and a favorable outcome to a live, multi-state legal fight over its newest and fastest-growing product. Position sizing, if this were ever added, should assume sharp moves around every court ruling, state enforcement action, and quarterly print.

What would move this from watchlist to portfolio consideration: (1) a Q3 2026 print confirming NFL-season event-contract revenue is tracking toward the roughly $326M H2 target, showing prediction markets aren't a one-event (World Cup) phenomenon; (2) a favorable Ninth Circuit ruling, or a critical mass of federal-court wins, that meaningfully de-risks the state-by-state legal threat to event contracts; (3) net interest and subscription revenue continuing to grow even if crypto stays depressed, proving the diversification claim holds under stress; and (4) either a valuation reset closer to traditional-broker multiples or a clear catalyst that justifies the premium Robinhood currently commands. Without at least one of these, the current risk/reward does not justify adding it to the fund over existing holdings.

Bottom Line: The Diversification Is Real. The Multiple Assumes It Stays That Way.

Robinhood in August 2026 is a materially better business than the meme-stock app of 2021: record revenue, record profit, 13 business lines above $100M in annualized revenue, and two genuine wealth-management footholds (Retirement, Advisor Network) it didn't have a few years ago. That's a real transformation, not just messaging.

But it's a transformation that's still running on trading volume. 59% of revenue is transaction-based, and the specific product driving that transaction revenue has quietly flipped from crypto (down 38% YoY) to prediction markets (up ~10x YoY) - a swap of one volatile, sentiment-driven revenue source for another one that is, additionally, tangled up in an active, unresolved legal fight across multiple states and courts. The stock's own 30% gap to its April highs, and analysts' unusually wide $57-$163.60 target range, suggest the market hasn't decided whether this is durable growth or a temporary sugar high either.

TEI positioning: Watchlist - not a current Growth Fund holding. If added, this would sit at Core tier within the Growth sleeve - the business is too large, profitable, and genuinely diversifying to size as a speculative Tail position, but the unresolved prediction-markets regulatory risk and a valuation that already prices in a lot of good news keep it below Anchor-level conviction next to the fund's existing highest-confidence holdings. TEI capital is currently better deployed in higher-conviction ideas already held in the fund - this is a capital-allocation call, not a rejection of the thesis. Monitor: the September 9 NFL kickoff and Q3 event-contract revenue, the Ninth Circuit jurisdiction ruling, and the Q3 2026 earnings print (expected late October) - whichever resolves the regulatory picture first.
